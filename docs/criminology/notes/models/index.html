Criminology Models

Models are simplified representations of criminological mechanisms. They help make assumptions explicit: who acts, what they choose, what they know, what they value, and how institutions respond.

The purpose of this section is not to make criminology artificially mathematical. The purpose is to use models as disciplined thinking tools.

Why use models?

A model helps answer questions such as:

  • Who are the actors?
  • What choices are available?
  • What information does each actor have?
  • What incentives or constraints matter?
  • What outcome should we expect?
  • What would change under a different policy?

A good model is not a full description of the world. It is a controlled way to isolate a mechanism.

Map of criminology models

These models connect directly to mechanisms. Deterrence can be modelled as inspection. Reporting can be modelled as cooperation under trust. Co-offending can be modelled as coordination. Crime diffusion can be modelled through networks.

From mechanism to model

A mechanism explains a process. A model formalises the process enough to examine its implications.

For example, deterrence as a mechanism can become an inspection game:

authority chooses monitor/not monitor
individual chooses offend/not offend
payoffs depend on gain, punishment, monitoring cost, and detection probability

This model can then ask how behaviour changes when monitoring becomes cheaper, punishment becomes harsher, or detection becomes more likely.

Matching models to questions

Research question Useful model
How does monitoring affect offending? Inspection game
Why do victims report or stay silent? Reporting and trust model
Why do offenders cooperate with one another? Co-offending game
How does crime spread through groups? Network crime model
Why do defendants accept plea deals? Plea bargaining model
How should police allocate scarce resources? Allocation dynamics model

The model should be chosen because it fits the mechanism, not because it looks sophisticated.

Actors and choices

Every model begins by identifying actors and choices.

Examples:

Model Actors Choices
Inspection game offender, authority offend/comply; inspect/not inspect
Reporting model victim/witness, police report/not report; respond/not respond
Co-offending game potential co-offenders join/not join; cooperate/betray
Plea bargaining prosecutor, defendant offer/accept/reject; trial/settle
Network model connected individuals offend, transmit information, recruit, withdraw
Allocation model enforcement agency, offenders allocate patrols; move, offend, avoid

A model is unclear if the actors and choices are unclear.

Information

Information is often the most important part of criminological modelling.

Actors may be uncertain about:

  • detection risk;
  • sanction severity;
  • police presence;
  • victim reporting;
  • co-offender loyalty;
  • evidence strength;
  • court outcomes;
  • institutional response;
  • peer behaviour;
  • market conditions.

Incomplete information can change the result completely. It can create signaling, screening, reputation, mistrust, overreaction, or hidden behaviour.

Payoffs and constraints

Payoffs do not need to mean money. In criminology, payoffs may include:

  • material gain;
  • physical risk;
  • punishment;
  • shame;
  • status;
  • revenge;
  • addiction;
  • fear;
  • stigma;
  • legal cost;
  • moral cost;
  • future opportunity;
  • institutional legitimacy.

A good model should avoid pretending that all motivations are the same. But it should still be clear about what is being assumed.

Timing

Timing determines the logic of the model.

For example, deterrence may look different if police move first, offenders move first, or both adapt over time.

Interpretation

The final step is interpretation. A model result should be translated back into criminological language.

A model may produce a result like:

\[ \Pr(\text{offend}) \text{ decreases when } \Pr(\text{detection}) \text{ increases}. \]

But the interpretation should ask:

  • Do people perceive detection risk accurately?
  • Which groups are most affected?
  • Does enforcement change reporting?
  • Does crime move elsewhere?
  • Does legitimacy rise or fall?
  • Are there unintended consequences?

Without interpretation, a model is only algebra.

Common mistakes

1. Adding too many features too early

A useful first model should be simple. Add complexity only when it clarifies the mechanism.

2. Forgetting the unit of analysis

A model of individual offending is not the same as a model of neighbourhood crime rates.

3. Treating payoffs as purely monetary

Status, fear, shame, addiction, trust, and identity may matter.

4. Ignoring institutions

Police, courts, prisons, regulators, and communities are strategic actors too.

5. Ignoring measurement

A model may predict true crime, but the available data may measure recorded crime.

6. Treating model results as automatic policy advice

A model clarifies one mechanism. Policy requires institutional, empirical, and ethical interpretation.

Checklist for building a criminology model

  1. What crime, behaviour, or institution is being studied?
  2. Who are the actors?
  3. What choices do they have?
  4. What information does each actor have?
  5. What are the payoffs, constraints, or motivations?
  6. What is the timing?
  7. Is the interaction one-shot, repeated, or networked?
  8. What solution concept is appropriate?
  9. What prediction does the model produce?
  10. What measurement problem might affect evidence?
  11. What would the model miss?
  12. What policy implication, if any, follows?
