Criminology Foundations

Foundations clarify the basic objects of criminological analysis: crime, deviance, harm, law, measurement, reporting, units of analysis, and explanation. These concepts matter because criminology often studies events that are partly hidden, socially interpreted, and institutionally recorded.

The goal of this section is to make the primitives clear before moving to mechanisms, institutions, models, or policy.

Why foundations matter

In criminology, the object being studied is rarely simple. A recorded crime is not only an event. It is also the result of reporting, classification, policing, and legal definition.

This means a criminological claim usually depends on both social reality and measurement process.

For example, a rise in recorded crime may reflect:

  • more actual offending;
  • more reporting;
  • changes in policing;
  • changes in legal classification;
  • changes in recording standards;
  • greater visibility of certain groups or places.

Crime, deviance, and harm

A behaviour can be harmful without being criminal. A behaviour can be criminal without being treated as equally harmful in practice. A behaviour can also be deviant without being illegal.

These distinctions are important.

Concept Basic meaning Example question
Crime Behaviour prohibited by criminal law What does the law define as punishable?
Deviance Behaviour violating social norms What does society label as unacceptable?
Harm Damage to persons, groups, or institutions Who is injured or made worse off?
Criminalization Process of making behaviour criminal Why is this behaviour treated as a criminal matter?
Social control Formal or informal regulation of behaviour Who enforces norms, and how?

Criminology often studies the relationship among these categories rather than assuming they are identical.

True crime and recorded crime

A central measurement problem is the gap between crime that occurs and crime that appears in official data.

This does not make official statistics useless. It means they should be interpreted carefully.

Official statistics are especially useful when paired with:

  • victimization surveys;
  • self-report surveys;
  • administrative records;
  • qualitative evidence;
  • local institutional knowledge;
  • careful comparison over time.

Units of analysis

Criminological research can focus on different units.

Unit Typical question
Individual Why does this person offend, desist, report, or comply?
Event Why did this crime happen here and now?
Place Why are some places crime hotspots?
Group How do peers, gangs, or organizations shape behaviour?
Network How do ties transmit information, opportunity, or trust?
Institution How do police, courts, prisons, or regulators act?
Society How do inequality, culture, law, and state capacity shape crime?

A theory that works at one level may not automatically work at another. For example, a theory of individual offending is not the same as a theory of neighbourhood crime rates.

From concept to measurement

A good empirical study must connect the concept being discussed to the variable being measured.

For example:

  • “fear of crime” may be measured by survey responses;
  • “police activity” may be measured by stops, arrests, patrol time, or spending;
  • “violence” may be measured by hospital admissions, police records, or victimization data;
  • “trust” may be measured by survey items, reporting behaviour, or cooperation.

Each measurement choice captures something slightly different.

Explanation versus description

Description answers:

What is happening?

Explanation asks:

Why is it happening?

A descriptive claim might say that burglary is concentrated in certain areas. An explanatory claim asks why those areas experience more burglary: opportunity, guardianship, poverty, housing structure, policing, social networks, or reporting practices.

The move from pattern to mechanism is where theory becomes important.

Causation and mechanism

A mechanism is a process that connects cause and effect.

For example:

poverty → stress and opportunity constraints → illegal income strategies

or:

low institutional trust → lower reporting → lower recorded crime

or:

increased guardianship → fewer suitable opportunities → lower burglary

A mechanism should make clear:

  1. what changes;
  2. who responds;
  3. why they respond;
  4. what outcome follows;
  5. what evidence would support or challenge the explanation.

Common measurement problems

1. Underreporting

Many crimes are never reported to authorities. This is especially important for domestic abuse, sexual violence, fraud, drug offences, and crimes involving distrust of police.

2. Recording variation

Even reported events may not be recorded consistently across time, place, or institution.

3. Detection bias

Some crimes are found only when institutions search for them. Drug offences, tax violations, and regulatory offences often depend heavily on enforcement intensity.

4. Classification changes

Legal or administrative changes can alter crime categories without any real change in behaviour.

5. Visibility bias

Highly visible populations and places may appear more criminal because they are more closely monitored.

6. Ecological fallacy

Patterns at the group or area level should not be automatically applied to individuals.

Checklist for foundation-level analysis

Before using a criminological claim, ask:

  1. What is being defined as crime?
  2. Is the issue crime, deviance, harm, or criminalization?
  3. What is the unit of analysis?
  4. Is the evidence about true crime or recorded crime?
  5. What measurement process produced the data?
  6. What mechanism is being proposed?
  7. What alternative explanations are possible?
  8. Does the claim confuse description with causation?
  9. Are institutions part of the measurement process?
  10. What would count as contrary evidence?
