Mechanisms

Mechanism pages explain how crime, compliance, reporting, punishment, and desistance are produced. Instead of treating theories as labels, this section treats them as channels: each mechanism describes a process linking context to behaviour.

A useful mechanism should answer:

What changes, who responds, why do they respond, and what outcome follows?

Mechanism-first criminology

A theory becomes analytically useful when it identifies a mechanism.

For example, deterrence is not just the claim that punishment reduces crime. The mechanism is that perceived sanction risk changes expected costs. If people do not perceive the risk, or if they are not responsive to future consequences, the mechanism may be weak.

Map of core mechanisms

These mechanisms can combine. A single crime pattern may involve opportunity, peer influence, weak guardianship, low trust, and institutional response at the same time.

Mechanisms and evidence

A mechanism should generate observable implications.

For example:

Mechanism Expected observable pattern
Deterrence Offending falls when perceived detection risk rises
Routine activity Crime clusters where targets and weak guardianship coincide
Peer effects Behaviour spreads through social ties or group settings
Legitimacy Cooperation increases when authorities are seen as fair
Labeling Formal contact may increase future exclusion or offending risk
Life-course Turning points alter offending trajectories
Self-control Immediate rewards weigh more heavily than delayed costs

Evidence does not need to be purely quantitative. Mechanisms can be studied through interviews, ethnography, administrative data, experiments, quasi-experiments, case studies, and formal modelling.

Mechanisms and models

Mechanisms can often be translated into simple models.

For example:

  • deterrence can become an inspection game;
  • reporting can become a trust and cooperation game;
  • co-offending can become a coordination game;
  • social diffusion can become a network model;
  • plea bargaining can become an asymmetric-information model.

This is why the mechanism section connects directly to the models section.

Mechanisms and policy

Policy works through mechanisms. A policy intervention does not simply “reduce crime” in the abstract. It changes incentives, opportunities, beliefs, constraints, or institutional behaviour.

Examples:

Policy type Mechanism it may target
More patrols Detection risk and guardianship
CCTV Visibility, deterrence, evidence generation
Youth programmes Peer networks, opportunity, self-control
Procedural justice training Legitimacy and trust
Rehabilitation skills, constraints, identity, and future orientation
Record expungement stigma and opportunity after system contact
Hotspot policing spatial concentration and resource allocation

A good policy analysis should ask which mechanism the policy is supposed to activate.

Common mistakes

1. Naming a theory instead of explaining it

Saying “this is deterrence” is not enough. Explain how perceived cost changes behaviour.

2. Assuming one mechanism explains everything

Most crime patterns are multi-mechanism. Violence, for example, may involve peers, alcohol, retaliation, opportunity, and low guardianship.

3. Ignoring measurement

If the outcome is recorded crime, the mechanism may affect reporting or detection rather than actual offending.

4. Treating policy as direct cause

Policy effects depend on institutional implementation and behavioural response.

5. Ignoring time

Some mechanisms are immediate, while others unfold across years.

6. Confusing individual and aggregate mechanisms

Individual-level explanations do not automatically explain area-level crime rates.
