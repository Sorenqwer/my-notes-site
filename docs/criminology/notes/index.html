Criminology Notes

These notes study crime, deviance, social control, and institutions through mechanisms. The aim is not only to list theories, but to ask how behaviour is produced, constrained, recorded, punished, justified, and changed.

The criminology section is organised around five layers:

  1. Foundations: core concepts and measurement problems.
  2. Mechanisms: causal and behavioural processes.
  3. Institutions: police, courts, corrections, and governance.
  4. Models: formal or semi-formal tools for thinking clearly.
  5. Topics: substantive crime areas such as violence, property crime, cybercrime, and drug markets.

The basic analytical chain

A useful criminological explanation usually connects context, mechanism, behaviour, measurement, and response.

This chain also shows where mistakes can occur. A change in recorded crime may reflect a change in actual offending, reporting, detection, classification, or enforcement intensity.

Crime as behaviour and record

One of the most important distinctions in criminology is the difference between true crime and recorded crime.

True crime refers to events that actually occur. Recorded crime refers to events that appear in official data.

These are not the same.

This distinction matters because many empirical claims rely on recorded data. A rise in recorded crime may mean more offending, better reporting, changed police practice, or changed classification.

Mechanisms before labels

A theory is most useful when it identifies a mechanism.

For example:

Theory family Possible mechanism
Deterrence Sanctions change expected costs
Routine activity Crime occurs when opportunity, target, and weak guardianship coincide
Self-control Impulsivity affects sensitivity to immediate reward
Social learning Behaviour spreads through peers and reinforcement
Labeling Official reaction changes identity and opportunity
Legitimacy Perceived fairness affects willingness to comply
Life-course theory Turning points change constraints and attachments

The same crime type can involve several mechanisms at once. Violent crime may involve self-control, retaliation, peer norms, alcohol, weapon access, and weak guardianship.

Institutions and incentives

Criminology is also about institutions. Police, courts, prisons, probation systems, regulators, communities, and firms all respond to crime under constraints.

Institutions face their own incentives:

  • limited budgets;
  • imperfect information;
  • political pressure;
  • legal constraints;
  • legitimacy concerns;
  • discretion;
  • error;
  • unequal visibility across groups and places.

A good institutional analysis asks not only whether an intervention works, but also how it changes incentives and behaviour across the system.

Why models are useful

Models do not replace empirical evidence or qualitative interpretation. They help clarify assumptions.

A model can show:

  • who the actors are;
  • what choices they face;
  • what information they have;
  • what incentives matter;
  • what outcome should be expected;
  • what would change under a policy intervention.

This is especially useful when the same policy may have different effects depending on reporting, deterrence, displacement, trust, and institutional response.

Example: deterrence as a mechanism

A simple deterrence argument says that offending becomes less attractive when expected punishment increases.

Expected punishment can be written roughly as

\[ \text{Expected punishment} = \Pr(\text{caught}) \times \text{sanction severity}. \]

A deterrence policy may increase either the probability of detection or the severity of punishment.

But the mechanism is not automatic. The effect depends on whether potential offenders perceive the risk, whether they are responsive to future consequences, whether opportunities remain available, and whether enforcement is legitimate.

This is why a simple model should be paired with institutional and behavioural interpretation.

Example: reporting and trust

Recorded crime depends partly on reporting. Reporting depends partly on trust.

This means recorded crime can rise even when true crime does not rise, if victims become more willing to report. It can also fall even when true crime does not fall, if trust declines.

Common mistakes

1. Treating recorded crime as true crime

Official statistics are valuable, but they are shaped by reporting, recording, policing, and classification.

2. Naming a theory without explaining the mechanism

A label is not an explanation. The mechanism should show how one thing produces another.

3. Ignoring institutions

Crime policy works through institutions, and institutions have incentives, constraints, and errors.

4. Assuming punishment works mechanically

Deterrence depends on perceived risk, responsiveness, legitimacy, and available alternatives.

5. Ignoring displacement

An intervention may move crime across time, space, target, method, or offence type.

6. Separating theory and measurement

The way crime is measured affects the conclusions that can be drawn from evidence.

How these notes connect to the rest of the site

Criminology connects naturally with game theory and psychology.

Game theory helps analyse enforcement, inspection, reporting, bargaining, cooperation, and strategic adaptation.

Psychology helps analyse impulse, identity, emotion, trauma, social cognition, moral judgement, and compliance.

Foundations help keep the reasoning clear: optimization for incentives, probability for uncertainty, dynamic reasoning for future consequences, and networks for social structure.
