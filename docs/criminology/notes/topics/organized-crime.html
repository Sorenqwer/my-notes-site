Organized Crime

Organized crime involves coordinated illegal activity that is structured, repeated, and often protected by reputation, violence, corruption, secrecy, or governance. It may involve illicit markets, extortion, trafficking, fraud, protection, smuggling, cybercrime, or control over territory and networks.

The key feature is organization under illegality. Since illegal agreements cannot rely on ordinary courts, organized crime often develops alternative enforcement mechanisms.

Basic mechanism map

Organized crime is not just “crime by groups.” It is crime where coordination, enforcement, and continuity matter.

Core mechanisms

Illegal governance

Organized groups may provide enforcement, protection, dispute resolution, and contract discipline in illegal markets.

Reputation

Reputation can substitute for legal enforcement. A group may maintain a reputation for violence or reliability to control behaviour.

Violence

Violence can be used to punish betrayal, deter rivals, discipline members, intimidate witnesses, or regulate markets.

Corruption

Corruption reduces enforcement risk, provides information, protects markets, or allows illegal activity to operate under legal cover.

Network resilience

Organized crime may survive enforcement because roles and ties can shift. Removing one actor may not destroy the structure.

Measurement issues

Organized crime is difficult to observe.

Issue Why it matters
Hidden structure membership and hierarchy are often unknown
Witness intimidation reporting and cooperation may be suppressed
Corruption institutional data may be compromised
Market secrecy transactions are hidden
Network adaptation groups reorganize after enforcement
Legal ambiguity some activities mix legal and illegal businesses

Data often come from investigations, prosecutions, intelligence, interviews, seizures, or network reconstruction. Each source is partial.

Repeated exchange model

Illegal markets face a problem: participants cannot use ordinary legal contract enforcement.

Repeated interaction can partly solve this.

The same mechanism that supports cooperation within the group can also produce violence against outsiders or defectors.

Corruption as protection

Corruption can be modelled as an exchange between criminal actors and officials.

Corruption changes expected costs and can make illegal markets more durable.

Policy trade-offs

Policy Mechanism Possible limitation
Arrest leaders disrupts hierarchy may create succession violence
Asset seizure reduces resources groups may hide assets
Anti-corruption enforcement raises protection cost politically difficult
Witness protection increases cooperation expensive and trust-dependent
Market disruption raises transaction cost may displace activity
Network targeting attacks central nodes network may adapt

Organized crime and violence

Violence may increase when markets are unstable. Strong groups may reduce visible violence by governing markets, while fragmented competition may increase conflict.

This creates a difficult policy issue:

This does not mean enforcement is bad. It means enforcement can have transitional effects that should be anticipated.

Common mistakes

1. Treating organized crime as a fixed hierarchy

Some groups are hierarchical, but others are networked, flexible, or project-based.

2. Ignoring governance

Organized crime may provide illegal order, not only disorder.

3. Treating violence as random

Violence may serve strategic functions such as discipline, reputation, and market control.

4. Ignoring corruption

Corruption can be central to durability.

5. Assuming removal of leaders ends the organization

Networks may adapt after leadership removal.

Checklist for analysing organized crime

  1. What activity or market is being organized?
  2. What roles exist?
  3. How are agreements enforced?
  4. What role does violence play?
  5. What role does corruption play?
  6. Is the structure hierarchical or networked?
  7. How does the group manage betrayal?
  8. How is the activity measured?
  9. What happens if enforcement disrupts the group?
  10. What unintended effects may follow?
