Drug Markets

Drug markets involve the production, distribution, sale, purchase, and consumption of prohibited or regulated substances. They combine demand, dependence, illicit exchange, enforcement risk, violence, public health, stigma, and institutional response.

Drug markets are useful for studying how policy changes market structure rather than simply turning behaviour on or off.

Basic mechanism map

The key feature of illicit drug markets is that demand and supply persist under legal risk, but the form of the market changes.

Core mechanisms

Demand persistence

Demand may persist because of dependence, recreation, pain, social environment, or economic opportunity. Enforcement alone may not remove demand.

Risk premium

Illegality raises costs and risks. Sellers may require higher returns to compensate for punishment risk, seizure, violence, and uncertainty.

Non-contractibility

Illegal market participants cannot rely on courts. This increases the importance of reputation, violence, trust, and repeated exchange.

Enforcement displacement

Enforcement may move markets across places, platforms, methods, or substances.

Public health harm

Drug policy affects not only crime, but also overdose, contamination, treatment access, stigma, and willingness to seek help.

Measurement issues

Drug markets are partly hidden and enforcement-dependent.

Issue Why it matters
Arrest data reflects enforcement intensity as well as behaviour
Seizure data reflects detection and policing strategy
Self-report data may understate stigmatized behaviour
Health data captures harm but not full market activity
Price and purity may indicate market pressure but are hard to measure
Open-air visibility visible markets are easier to police and count

A rise in drug arrests may mean more drug activity, more enforcement, or changed policing priorities.

Illicit market logic

A simple market view begins with supply and demand, but illegality adds enforcement risk.

Enforcement can raise costs, but the effect depends on market adaptation and demand elasticity.

Violence in drug markets

Violence may arise because illegal market participants cannot use legal institutions to resolve disputes.

Violence is therefore connected to governance, trust, and market structure.

Enforcement and adaptation

Drug markets often adapt to enforcement.

This means policy analysis should look beyond immediate arrests.

Public health approach

A public health approach focuses on reducing harm, not only reducing supply.

Possible mechanisms include:

  • treatment access;
  • harm reduction;
  • overdose prevention;
  • safer consumption information;
  • reduced stigma;
  • housing and support;
  • substitution treatment;
  • early intervention.

This approach changes the outcome of interest from “number of arrests” to harm, survival, treatment, and stability.

Policy trade-offs

Policy Intended mechanism Possible risk
Supply enforcement raises cost and risk displacement, violence, hidden markets
Demand reduction lowers consumption slow and support-dependent
Treatment reduces dependence-related demand access and retention challenges
Harm reduction reduces death and injury political resistance
Decriminalization reduces justice-system harm depends on health infrastructure
Market regulation reduces illicit control design and compliance challenges

Common mistakes

1. Treating drug markets as simple supply problems

Demand, dependence, substitution, and social context matter.

2. Using arrests as direct evidence of market size

Arrests are strongly shaped by enforcement.

3. Ignoring public health outcomes

Policy may reduce visible dealing but increase overdose or contamination risk.

4. Ignoring displacement

Markets can move across space, method, or substance.

5. Treating all substances as the same

Different drugs have different markets, harms, users, and enforcement dynamics.

Checklist for analysing drug markets

  1. Which substance or market is being studied?
  2. What drives demand?
  3. How is supply organized?
  4. How does enforcement affect risk and price?
  5. What role does violence play?
  6. What data source is being used?
  7. Does the measure reflect behaviour or enforcement?
  8. Is displacement likely?
  9. What public health outcomes matter?
  10. What policy trade-off is involved?
