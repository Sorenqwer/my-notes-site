How criminology is studied

Scope

Criminology is not only “crime statistics.” As an academic field, it combines: - Concepts and measurement (what is counted as crime; why measurement is difficult) - Theories of mechanism (why behavior occurs; how social structure matters) - Institutional analysis (police/courts/corrections; legitimacy and compliance) - Evaluation and identification (what changes outcomes; how we infer causality)

This page provides a discipline map so readers can locate a topic within the broader research program.

Core question types

1) Descriptive (pattern-finding)

Typical objects: - concentration by person (repeat offending/victimization), - concentration by place (hotspots), - temporal patterns (seasonality, routine changes), - network clustering (co-offending, gangs).

The descriptive layer also flags the measurement problem: recorded crime depends on reporting and enforcement.

2) Explanatory / causal (mechanism)

Mechanism-based questions: - How do incentives and detection risk affect offending? - When does opportunity/guardianship reduce crime without displacement? - How do peer effects and networks amplify or dampen crime? - How does legitimacy affect reporting and compliance?

3) Evaluative / policy (interventions)

Interventions include: - policing strategies (hotspots, visibility, patrol allocation), - sanctions and sentencing policies, - prevention programs, - rehabilitation and reentry policies, - institutional reforms targeting trust and cooperation.

Levels of analysis (what unit the theory “lives on”)

Criminological explanations often differ by unit:

  • Individual: preferences, beliefs, constraints, self-control, human capital
  • Interaction / network: strategic complements, trust, co-offending, diffusion
  • Place: opportunities, guardianship, environmental design, spatial displacement
  • Institution: rules, legitimacy, capacity, incentives within organizations

A good academic write-up states its unit explicitly and clarifies what is taken as primitive.

Evidence types (what “counts” in the literature)

Even if you focus on theory, it helps to know what kinds of evidence usually support claims:

  • Administrative data: police incidents, arrests, court records, corrections
  • Surveys / self-reports: victimization surveys, self-reported offending
  • Field experiments / RCTs: randomized patrol or program assignment
  • Quasi-experiments: policy changes, discontinuities, natural experiments
  • Qualitative: interviews, ethnography, case studies (mechanism discovery)

Common threats to inference (why crime data is hard)

These issues appear repeatedly in criminology papers:

  • Underreporting (many crimes never enter official data)
  • Enforcement selection (arrests reflect policing choices)
  • Simultaneity (police respond to crime; crime responds to police)
  • Displacement (crime moves across space/time/type)
  • Equilibrium adaptation (offenders change tactics when policy changes)

For theory, these threats matter because they influence what is “observable” and how one interprets patterns.

Theory families (mechanism organizers)

A practical academic taxonomy:

  1. Incentives / deterrence
  2. Opportunity / routine activity
  3. Social interactions and networks
  4. Legitimacy, trust, and compliance
  5. Inequality, deprivation, and social capital
  6. Life-course dynamics and stigma
  7. Behavioral microfoundations (present bias, impulsivity; optional)

These map cleanly into models: inspection games, bargaining, dynamic choice, network games.

How to read a criminology paper (fast checklist)

When skimming: 1) What is the unit of analysis? (person/place/network/institution)
2) What outcome is used? (incidence vs recorded vs victimization)
3) What mechanism is proposed?
4) What is the identification strategy (if empirical)?
5) What equilibrium or adaptation effects could offset the policy?
6) What predictions are robust across plausible measurement choices?
