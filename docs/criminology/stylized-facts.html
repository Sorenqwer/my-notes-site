Stylized facts

Scope

This page lists stylized facts: recurring empirical regularities widely discussed in criminology and related social-science literatures.
They are not meant as a complete empirical review, but as constraints for theory: mechanisms and models should be able to reproduce (at least qualitatively) these patterns.

A recurring caution: many “crime facts” depend on measurement (reporting, enforcement, recording practices). When relevant, the fact is stated as a pattern about recorded crime, victimization, or behavior.

1) Concentration in people and places

  • A small share of people account for a large share of offending (repeat offending / persistence).
    Theory implication: heterogeneity and dynamics (states, types, or learning) matter.
  • Crime concentrates spatially (hotspots: small areas produce disproportionate incidents).
    Theory implication: opportunity and guardianship are place-specific; equilibrium responses can relocate activity.

(Links that will eventually connect) - Opportunity / routine activity → notes/mechanisms/opportunity-routine.qmd - Policing allocation / hotspots → notes/institutions/policing.qmd

2) Recorded crime is not the same as incidence

  • Not all victimization is reported, and reporting varies by offense type, victim characteristics, and trust in institutions.
    Theory implication: include a reporting stage; observed data may reflect participation constraints.
  • Enforcement is selective: arrest/case data reflect policing choices and capacity, not just offending.
    Theory implication: strategic interaction can change observables even when underlying behavior changes less.

(Links) - Legitimacy & compliance → notes/mechanisms/legitimacy-compliance.qmd - Reporting & trust model → notes/models/reporting-trust.qmd

3) Opportunity and routine predict timing

  • Crime risk often shifts with routine activity patterns (crowding, guardianship, mobility, visibility).
    Theory implication: “technology of opportunity” matters; policies can change opportunity without changing preferences.

(Links) - Opportunity / routine activity → notes/mechanisms/opportunity-routine.qmd

4) Sanctions: detection vs severity (and strategic adaptation)

  • The deterrent effect of punishment is often discussed as depending on both probability of detection and severity of sanction; in practice, offenders may adapt (switch targets, tactics, locations, or timing).
    Theory implication: model deterrence as a game (inspection/monitoring) and allow substitution across actions.

(Links) - Incentives & deterrence → notes/mechanisms/incentives-deterrence.qmd - Inspection games → notes/models/inspection-games.qmd

5) Social interactions and networks matter

  • Offending can be socially embedded: co-offending, peer effects, clustering within networks, diffusion of tactics.
    Theory implication: strategic complements, learning, and partner choice can generate clustering and persistence.

(Links) - Social interactions → notes/mechanisms/social-interactions.qmd - Co-offending games → notes/models/co-offending-games.qmd - Network crime → notes/models/network-crime.qmd

6) Inequality, deprivation, and trust appear repeatedly

  • Many literatures link violence and disorder to deprivation, inequality, and low social trust/collective efficacy (interpretations differ across theories).
    Theory implication: these factors can enter as outside options, returns to legitimate work, enforcement cooperation, or social capital.

(Links) - Inequality & trust → notes/mechanisms/inequality-trust.qmd

7) Life-course dynamics: onset and desistance

  • Offending is often described as following a life-course pattern (onset, persistence, desistance), with strong roles for transitions (schooling, work, relationships) and system contact (stigma).
    Theory implication: dynamic incentives and state dependence; consider reputational/stigma channels.

(Links) - Life-course dynamics → notes/mechanisms/life-course.qmd

8) Institutions are strategic actors, not passive rules

  • Policing, courts, and corrections have objectives, constraints, and incentives; policy changes can produce equilibrium responses (by offenders, victims, and agencies).
    Theory implication: model institutions as players with limited capacity and endogenous targeting.

Then state: which outcomes are “behavior” vs “recorded data”, and where measurement enters.

Minimal “theory constraints” (what your models should be able to explain)

A theory-first criminology note set is especially strong if it can generate: - concentration (people/places), - substitution/adaptation under enforcement, - the role of reporting and legitimacy, - network clustering/co-offending, - dynamics (persistence and desistance).
