Methods Map

A method should match the research question. Criminology uses many kinds of evidence because crime is partly hidden, institutionally recorded, socially interpreted, and unevenly distributed.

This page maps common research questions to suitable methods.

The basic decision

The first question is not “Which method should I use?” but:

What kind of question am I asking?

Descriptive questions

Descriptive questions ask:

What is happening?

Examples:

  • How has recorded burglary changed over time?
  • Which areas have high rates of violent crime?
  • Which groups report lower trust in police?
  • How do case outcomes vary across courts?

Useful methods include:

  • summary statistics;
  • rates and ratios;
  • time-series plots;
  • maps;
  • cross-tabulations;
  • administrative data summaries;
  • victimization survey summaries.

Description is not weak. Good description is often the foundation for good explanation.

Mechanism questions

Mechanism questions ask:

How or why does this pattern arise?

Examples:

  • Why might victims choose not to report?
  • How do peer groups shape offending?
  • How does labeling affect future opportunity?
  • Why might aggressive enforcement reduce trust?

Useful methods include:

  • qualitative interviews;
  • ethnography;
  • case studies;
  • process tracing;
  • formal modelling;
  • theory-guided empirical analysis;
  • mixed-method designs.

Mechanism evidence often needs more than one source.

Causal questions

Causal questions ask:

What is the effect of X on Y?

Examples:

  • Does hotspot policing reduce crime?
  • Does incarceration reduce future offending?
  • Does CCTV reduce theft?
  • Does procedural justice training improve trust?
  • Does a sentencing reform change prison populations?

Useful methods include:

  • randomized experiments;
  • natural experiments;
  • difference-in-differences;
  • regression discontinuity;
  • instrumental variables;
  • matching;
  • panel models.

Causal designs need a credible comparison. The key problem is usually the counterfactual:

What would have happened without the treatment?

Prediction questions

Prediction questions ask:

Can we forecast risk or classify outcomes?

Examples:

  • Which locations are at higher risk of burglary?
  • Which cases are likely to drop out?
  • Which offenders are likely to reoffend?
  • Which online accounts are likely to be fraudulent?

Useful methods include:

  • logistic regression;
  • decision trees;
  • random forests;
  • gradient boosting;
  • survival models;
  • validation and calibration checks;
  • error analysis.

Prediction is not the same as causal explanation. A variable may predict an outcome without causing it.

Interpretive questions

Interpretive questions ask:

What meaning do actors attach to behaviour, institutions, or experiences?

Examples:

  • How do young people understand police stops?
  • How do victims interpret justice?
  • How do offenders justify rule-breaking?
  • How do practitioners understand discretion?

Useful methods include:

  • interviews;
  • focus groups;
  • ethnography;
  • discourse analysis;
  • document analysis;
  • thematic coding.

Interpretive work is especially useful when the research question concerns meaning, identity, legitimacy, fear, stigma, or experience.

Evaluation questions

Evaluation questions ask:

Did a programme or policy work, how did it work, and at what cost?

Examples:

  • Did an intervention reduce reoffending?
  • Was the programme implemented as intended?
  • Did the policy create displacement?
  • Did the benefits justify the cost?
  • Did the intervention affect groups unequally?

Useful methods include:

  • process evaluation;
  • outcome evaluation;
  • impact evaluation;
  • cost-benefit analysis;
  • implementation analysis;
  • mixed-method evaluation.

A strong evaluation separates:

  1. whether the programme was implemented;
  2. whether outcomes changed;
  3. whether the programme caused the change;
  4. whether the change was worth the cost.

Data sources

Criminology uses multiple data sources.

Data source Strength Limitation
Police records detailed official events affected by reporting and recording
Court records legal process and outcomes only includes cases entering courts
Prison/probation records correctional contact selected population
Victimization surveys captures unreported victimization recall and survey design issues
Self-report surveys captures hidden offending honesty and sample issues
Interviews meaning and mechanism limited generalizability
Ethnography deep contextual insight time-intensive and local
Spatial data place concentration boundary and exposure issues
Network data relational structure missing ties and privacy constraints
Digital trace data scale and behaviour logs platform bias and access limits

No data source is perfect. The method should acknowledge what the data can and cannot show.

Matching table

Question type Example question Suitable methods
Descriptive Where is crime concentrated? maps, rates, trend plots
Mechanism Why do victims not report? interviews, process tracing, reporting model
Causal Did the policy reduce crime? experiment, DiD, RD, IV
Predictive Who is at high risk? machine learning, validation, calibration
Interpretive How do people experience policing? interviews, ethnography
Institutional How does the court process cases? administrative data, process evaluation
Network How does crime diffuse? network analysis, diffusion models

Common mistakes

1. Using a causal language with descriptive evidence

A correlation or trend does not automatically identify an effect.

2. Treating prediction as explanation

A predictive variable is not necessarily a causal mechanism.

3. Ignoring measurement process

Crime data are produced through reporting, detection, and recording.

4. Choosing method before question

The research question should determine the method.

5. Ignoring implementation

A policy cannot work as designed if it was not implemented as designed.

6. Overgeneralizing from one data source

Different sources capture different parts of the crime process.

Method-selection checklist

  1. What is the research question?
  2. Is it descriptive, causal, predictive, interpretive, institutional, or mechanism-focused?
  3. What is the unit of analysis?
  4. What is the outcome?
  5. Is the outcome true crime, recorded crime, reporting, detection, or institutional response?
  6. What comparison is needed?
  7. What data source is available?
  8. What does the data miss?
  9. What alternative explanations remain?
  10. What kind of claim can the method support?
