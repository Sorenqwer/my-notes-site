Modeling Checklist

This checklist helps turn a criminological question into a simple model. The goal is not to force every topic into mathematics. The goal is to make assumptions explicit.

A model should clarify actors, choices, information, incentives, timing, and expected outcomes.

The basic modelling chain

A weak model skips steps. A strong model makes each step visible.

Step 1: Define the problem

Start with the behaviour or institution you want to explain.

Examples:

  • Why do some victims not report crime?
  • How does monitoring affect rule-breaking?
  • Why do co-offenders cooperate?
  • How do police allocate patrols?
  • Why do defendants accept plea bargains?
  • How does cybercrime scale across platforms?

Write the problem in one sentence:

I want to explain why [actor] chooses [action] under [condition].

Step 2: Identify actors

Actors are the decision-makers in the model.

Possible actors include:

  • offender;
  • victim;
  • witness;
  • police;
  • prosecutor;
  • defendant;
  • judge;
  • prison authority;
  • regulator;
  • firm;
  • peer group;
  • community;
  • platform;
  • criminal organization.

A model should not include every possible actor. Include actors only if their choices are central to the mechanism.

Step 3: Define choices

Each actor needs a choice set.

Examples:

Actor Possible choices
offender offend / not offend
police inspect / not inspect
victim report / not report
prosecutor charge / dismiss / bargain
defendant accept plea / reject plea
co-offender cooperate / betray
regulator audit / not audit
platform moderate / ignore / report

Keep the first version small. A two-action model is often enough to clarify the mechanism.

Step 4: Specify information

Ask what each actor knows when choosing.

Information may include:

  • detection probability;
  • sanction severity;
  • police presence;
  • evidence strength;
  • co-offender reliability;
  • institutional trust;
  • victim willingness to report;
  • type of offender;
  • strength of social ties;
  • platform rules;
  • legal consequences.

If actors are uncertain, beliefs must be part of the model.

Step 5: Define payoffs

Payoffs represent what actors care about.

In criminology, payoffs can include:

  • money;
  • status;
  • revenge;
  • fear;
  • shame;
  • punishment;
  • time;
  • effort;
  • moral cost;
  • legal risk;
  • stigma;
  • trust;
  • safety;
  • future opportunity;
  • institutional legitimacy.

Do not assume payoffs are purely financial unless the model requires it.

Step 6: Choose timing

Timing determines the model type.

Examples:

  • Offender and police choose simultaneously: inspection game.
  • Offender acts, victim reports, police respond: sequential game.
  • Community and police interact repeatedly: repeated trust game.
  • Offending spreads through peers: network model.

Step 7: Choose the solution concept or prediction

The solution concept depends on the model.

Model type Typical solution or prediction
One-person choice model optimal action
Simultaneous game Nash equilibrium
Sequential game backward induction or subgame perfection
Incomplete-information game Bayesian reasoning
Repeated game cooperation, punishment, reputation
Network model diffusion, centrality, clustering
Empirical prediction model validation and error analysis

Not every model needs a formal equilibrium. Sometimes the purpose is to clarify a mechanism or generate testable predictions.

Step 8: Interpret the result

A model result should be translated back into criminological language.

For example, if the model says:

offending falls when detection probability rises

then interpret:

  • Do actors perceive detection risk?
  • Is enforcement evenly distributed?
  • Does crime displace?
  • Does trust change?
  • Does recorded crime rise because detection rises?
  • What are the unintended consequences?

Interpretation prevents the model from becoming detached from reality.

Step 9: Connect to evidence

Every model should suggest observable implications.

For example:

Model claim Observable implication
deterrence works through perceived risk perceived risk should change before behaviour changes
trust affects reporting low-trust groups should report less, holding victimization constant
peer effects matter behaviour should cluster through social ties
stigma affects reoffending formal contact should affect later opportunity
displacement occurs crime should move across place, time, target, or method

Example: reporting model

Problem:

Why might victims not report crime?

Actors:

  • victim;
  • police or institution.

Victim choices:

  • report;
  • do not report.

Information:

  • expected police response;
  • perceived cost of reporting;
  • trust in institution;
  • fear of retaliation;
  • seriousness of harm.

Payoffs:

report if expected benefit of reporting > expected cost of reporting

Mechanism:

Prediction:

  • higher trust should increase reporting;
  • higher stigma or retaliation risk should reduce reporting;
  • recorded crime may rise if reporting increases.

Example: inspection game

Problem:

How does monitoring affect rule-breaking?

Actors:

  • potential offender;
  • authority.

Choices:

  • offender: offend or comply;
  • authority: inspect or not inspect.

Information:

  • offender may not know whether inspection occurs;
  • authority may not know whether offending occurs.

Payoffs:

  • offending gives gain if undetected;
  • inspection is costly;
  • punishment reduces payoff if detected.

Mechanism:

Prediction:

  • higher perceived inspection risk should reduce offending;
  • higher inspection may increase recorded crime in the short run;
  • offenders may adapt if monitoring is predictable.

Common mistakes

1. Including too many actors

Start simple. Add actors only when they change the mechanism.

2. Making choices vague

Choices should be specific enough to analyse.

3. Ignoring information

Strategic behaviour depends heavily on what actors know or believe.

4. Treating payoffs as only money

Status, fear, legitimacy, shame, and time may matter.

5. Confusing model outcome with data

The model may predict true offending, while the data may measure recorded crime.

6. Jumping from model to policy

Policy requires evidence, implementation, ethics, and institutional feasibility.

Checklist

Use this checklist before writing a model page:

  1. What is the behaviour or institution being explained?
  2. Who are the actors?
  3. What choices do they have?
  4. What does each actor know?
  5. What is uncertain?
  6. What beliefs matter?
  7. What does each actor value?
  8. What are the constraints?
  9. What is the timing?
  10. Is the interaction one-shot, repeated, or networked?
  11. What solution concept or prediction is appropriate?
  12. What observable implication follows?
  13. What data could test or illustrate the prediction?
  14. What does the model ignore?
  15. What policy implication is safe to draw?
