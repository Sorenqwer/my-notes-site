Probability

Probability is the language of uncertainty. In game theory, it appears whenever players randomise, hold beliefs, observe signals, face private information, or make decisions under incomplete knowledge.

This page introduces the probability tools most often used in the notes: events, random variables, expectation, mixed strategies, conditional probability, Bayes’ rule, signals, types, and posterior beliefs.

Why probability matters in game theory

Game theory uses probability in two main ways.

First, probability can describe randomisation. A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over actions.

Second, probability can describe beliefs. In Bayesian games, players may be uncertain about another player’s type, payoff, information, or intention.

The same mathematical tool therefore has two interpretations: either a player is genuinely randomising, or another player is uncertain about what is true.

Events and probabilities

A sample space is the set of possible outcomes. An event is a subset of the sample space.

For example, if a player may be either high-cost or low-cost, the sample space is

\[ \Omega = \{H,L\}. \]

The event that the player is high-cost is

\[ \{H\}. \]

A probability assigns a number between 0 and 1 to each event. If

\[ \mathbb{P}(H)=0.3, \]

then the probability of type \(H\) is 0.3.

Since there are only two types here,

\[ \mathbb{P}(L)=1-\mathbb{P}(H)=0.7. \]

Random variables

A random variable assigns numerical values to outcomes.

For example, suppose a player’s cost can be high or low:

\[ c(H)=10, \qquad c(L)=4. \]

If the probability of type \(H\) is 0.3 and the probability of type \(L\) is 0.7, then the cost is uncertain before the type is known.

Random variables are useful because payoffs often depend on uncertain states, types, signals, or actions.

Expectation

If a random variable \(X\) takes values \(x_k\) with probabilities \(p_k\), then

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_k x_k p_k. \]

Expectation is the probability-weighted average value of a random variable.

For example, if

\[ X = \begin{cases} 10 & \text{with probability } 0.3,\\ 4 & \text{with probability } 0.7, \end{cases} \]

then

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = 10(0.3)+4(0.7) = 5.8. \]

In games, expected utility uses exactly this logic: average payoffs over possible outcomes, weighted by their probabilities.

Expected utility

Suppose a player chooses action \(a\), but the payoff depends on an uncertain state \(\theta\). If the possible states are \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_K\), expected utility is

\[ \mathbb{E}[u(a,\theta)] = \sum_{k=1}^{K} u(a,\theta_k)\mathbb{P}(\theta_k). \]

The player chooses the action with the highest expected utility:

\[ a^* \in \arg\max_{a \in A} \sum_{k=1}^{K} u(a,\theta_k)\mathbb{P}(\theta_k). \]

This is the bridge between probability and optimization.

Mixed strategies

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure actions.

If a player has two actions, \(A\) and \(B\), then a mixed strategy may be written as

\[ \sigma(A)=p, \qquad \sigma(B)=1-p, \]

where

\[ p \in [0,1]. \]

The player chooses \(A\) with probability \(p\) and \(B\) with probability \(1-p\).

If the action set is finite,

\[ S_i = \{s_{i1},s_{i2},\ldots,s_{im}\}, \]

then a mixed strategy is a probability vector

\[ \sigma_i = (\sigma_i(s_{i1}),\ldots,\sigma_i(s_{im})), \]

where

\[ \sigma_i(s_{ij}) \geq 0 \]

for all \(j\), and

\[ \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_i(s_{ij})=1. \]

Mixed strategies are not mystical. They are probability distributions that allow players and opponents to compute expected payoffs.

Expected payoff under a mixed strategy

Suppose player 1 chooses between \(A\) and \(B\), while player 2 chooses between \(L\) and \(R\).

If player 2 plays \(L\) with probability \(q\) and \(R\) with probability \(1-q\), then player 1’s expected payoff from playing \(A\) is

\[ U_1(A,q) = q u_1(A,L) + (1-q)u_1(A,R). \]

Player 1’s expected payoff from playing \(B\) is

\[ U_1(B,q) = q u_1(B,L) + (1-q)u_1(B,R). \]

Player 1 prefers \(A\) when

\[ U_1(A,q)>U_1(B,q). \]

Player 1 prefers \(B\) when

\[ U_1(B,q)>U_1(A,q). \]

Player 1 is indifferent when

\[ U_1(A,q)=U_1(B,q). \]

This indifference condition is often used to solve mixed-strategy equilibria.

Conditional probability

Conditional probability asks: what is the probability of event \(A\) given that event \(B\) has occurred?

It is written as

\[ \mathbb{P}(A \mid B). \]

For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(\mathbb{P}(B)>0\),

\[ \mathbb{P}(A\mid B) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}. \]

This formula says:

among the cases where \(B\) occurs, what fraction also involve \(A\)?

Conditional probability is essential for belief updating.

Bayes’ rule

Bayes’ rule rewrites conditional probability in a form useful for updating beliefs.

For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(\mathbb{P}(B)>0\),

\[ \mathbb{P}(A\mid B) = \frac{ \mathbb{P}(B\mid A)\mathbb{P}(A) }{ \mathbb{P}(B) }. \]

The terms have a standard interpretation:

Term Meaning
\(\mathbb{P}(A)\) prior belief
\(\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)\) likelihood of observing evidence \(B\) if \(A\) is true
\(\mathbb{P}(B)\) overall probability of observing evidence \(B\)
\(\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)\) posterior belief after observing \(B\)

The intuition is:

prior belief is reweighted by how well the hypothesis predicts the evidence.

Bayes’ rule is especially important in games with incomplete information, where players must update beliefs after observing signals, messages, or actions.

Belief updating as a process

In words:

  1. Start with a prior belief.
  2. Observe a signal.
  3. Ask how likely the signal would be under each possible state.
  4. Reweight the prior.
  5. Use the posterior belief to choose an action.

Example: updating beliefs about types

A player is either type \(H\) or type \(L\). The prior beliefs are

\[ \mathbb{P}(H)=0.3, \qquad \mathbb{P}(L)=0.7. \]

You observe a signal \(s\). Suppose

\[ \mathbb{P}(s\mid H)=0.8, \qquad \mathbb{P}(s\mid L)=0.2. \]

First compute the overall probability of observing \(s\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(s) = \mathbb{P}(s\mid H)\mathbb{P}(H) + \mathbb{P}(s\mid L)\mathbb{P}(L). \]

Substitute the numbers:

\[ \mathbb{P}(s) = 0.8 \cdot 0.3 + 0.2 \cdot 0.7 = 0.24+0.14 = 0.38. \]

Then compute the posterior probability of type \(H\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(H\mid s) = \frac{ \mathbb{P}(s\mid H)\mathbb{P}(H) }{ \mathbb{P}(s) } = \frac{0.8 \cdot 0.3}{0.38} = \frac{0.24}{0.38} \approx 0.6316. \]

So after observing signal \(s\), belief in type \(H\) rises from 0.3 to about 0.63.

The signal increases belief in \(H\) because it is more likely under \(H\) than under \(L\).

Prior, likelihood, posterior

The belief-updating example can be summarised as:

A posterior belief is not just the prior plus the signal. It depends on how diagnostic the signal is.

A signal is more diagnostic when its likelihood differs strongly across states.

Signals and types

In Bayesian games, a type represents private information about a player. A type may describe:

  • cost
  • ability
  • patience
  • preference
  • information
  • intention
  • valuation
  • honesty or bias

A signal is something observed that may contain information about the type.

The basic structure is:

This structure appears in signaling games, cheap-talk games, reputation models, screening problems, and many models of trust.

Bayesian games

A Bayesian game includes uncertainty about types. The model normally specifies:

  1. a set of players;
  2. a type space for each player;
  3. prior beliefs over types;
  4. actions or strategies;
  5. payoffs that may depend on types.

A player’s strategy must specify what they do for each possible type. If player \(i\) has type \(\theta_i\), a strategy can be written as

\[ s_i(\theta_i). \]

A Bayesian Nash equilibrium requires each type to choose a best response given beliefs and the strategies of others.

In short:

\[ \text{Bayesian reasoning} = \text{optimization under beliefs}. \]

Beliefs after actions

In dynamic games, players may update beliefs after observing actions.

For example, suppose a sender chooses a message \(m\), and the receiver must infer the sender’s type \(\theta\).

The receiver’s posterior belief is

\[ \mu(\theta \mid m). \]

Using Bayes’ rule,

\[ \mu(\theta \mid m) = \frac{ \mathbb{P}(m\mid \theta)\mathbb{P}(\theta) }{ \sum_{\theta'} \mathbb{P}(m\mid \theta')\mathbb{P}(\theta') }. \]

This formula says that a message changes beliefs according to how likely each type would be to send that message.

This is the foundation of signaling and cheap talk.

On-path and off-path beliefs

In dynamic games with incomplete information, some actions or messages occur with positive probability in equilibrium. These are called on-path.

Other actions or messages are not expected to occur in equilibrium. These are called off-path.

Bayes’ rule pins down beliefs after on-path observations. But after an off-path observation, Bayes’ rule may not give a unique answer because the denominator may be zero.

This is why solution concepts such as perfect Bayesian equilibrium and sequential equilibrium include belief requirements.

The key idea is:

Independence

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if learning that one occurred does not change the probability of the other.

Formally,

\[ \mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \mathbb{P}(A\cap B) = \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B). \]

Independence is useful, but it should not be assumed automatically. In strategic settings, actions are often informative precisely because they are not independent of types or incentives.

Common mistakes

1. Confusing prior and posterior

The prior is the belief before observing evidence. The posterior is the belief after updating on evidence.

2. Ignoring the denominator in Bayes’ rule

The denominator \(\mathbb{P}(B)\) matters because it measures how likely the evidence is overall.

3. Treating a signal as proof

A signal changes beliefs. It does not usually reveal the state with certainty.

4. Assuming independence without justification

If actions are strategically chosen, they may be correlated with private information.

5. Confusing mixed strategies with uncertainty about pure strategies

A mixed strategy is a deliberate probability distribution over actions. Uncertainty about what someone will do may arise from mixed strategies, private information, or lack of observation.

6. Forgetting off-path beliefs

In dynamic incomplete-information games, behaviour after unexpected observations may matter for equilibrium.

Checklist for belief updating

When using Bayes’ rule in a model, ask:

  1. What are the possible states or types?
  2. What is the prior belief over those states?
  3. What signal or action is observed?
  4. How likely is that signal under each state?
  5. What is the overall probability of the signal?
  6. What is the posterior belief after observing it?
  7. How does the posterior belief affect the next action?
  8. Are we updating after an on-path or off-path observation?
