Optimization

Optimization is the language of choice. In economics and game theory, many arguments begin with a simple question:

Given the available options, which choice gives the highest payoff, utility, profit, or value?

This page collects the basic optimization tools needed for strategic reasoning: objective functions, constraints, argmax notation, first-order conditions, second-order conditions, and constrained optimization.

The basic structure

An optimization problem has three parts:

  1. Choice variable: what the decision-maker controls.
  2. Objective function: what the decision-maker wants to maximize or minimize.
  3. Feasible set: which choices are allowed.

A simple maximization problem can be written as

\[ \max_{x \in X} f(x), \]

where \(x\) is the choice variable, \(X\) is the feasible set, and \(f(x)\) is the objective function.

The solution is written as

\[ x^* \in \arg\max_{x \in X} f(x). \]

This means that \(x^*\) is a choice that achieves the highest value of \(f(x)\) among all feasible choices.

The logic of optimization

Many problems in economics and game theory can be organised as an optimization problem.

The workflow matters because many mistakes come from applying calculus before defining the feasible set.

Argmax notation

The notation

\[ \arg\max_{x \in X} f(x) \]

means “the value or values of \(x\) that maximize \(f(x)\) over the set \(X\).”

This is different from

\[ \max_{x \in X} f(x). \]

The expression \(\max f(x)\) gives the maximum value of the objective function.
The expression \(\arg\max f(x)\) gives the choice that achieves that value.

For example, suppose

\[ f(x)=2x-x^2. \]

The maximum occurs at \(x=1\). The maximum value is

\[ f(1)=2(1)-1^2=1. \]

So:

\[ \arg\max_x f(x)=1, \]

but

\[ \max_x f(x)=1. \]

In this example the argmax and the maximum value happen to be numerically the same, but conceptually they are different.

Interior and boundary solutions

A solution is an interior solution if it lies strictly inside the feasible set. A solution is a boundary solution if it occurs at the edge of the feasible set.

This distinction matters because first-order conditions usually identify interior candidates. Boundary solutions may not satisfy the usual condition \(f'(x)=0\).

For example, consider

\[ \max_{x \in [0,1]} x. \]

The solution is

\[ x^*=1. \]

But

\[ f'(x)=1, \]

so there is no point where \(f'(x)=0\). The optimum is at the boundary.

First-order condition

If \(f\) is differentiable and \(x^*\) is an interior optimum, then the first-order condition is

\[ f'(x^*)=0. \]

The intuition is that, at an interior optimum, a small movement to the left or right should not improve the objective.

For example, suppose

\[ f(x)=2x-x^2. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=2-2x. \]

Set the derivative equal to zero:

\[ 2-2x=0. \]

So

\[ x^*=1. \]

The first-order condition gives a candidate optimum.

Second-order condition

The first-order condition alone is not enough. It finds stationary points, but a stationary point may be a maximum, a minimum, or neither.

If \(f\) is twice differentiable, the second-order condition for a local maximum is

\[ f''(x^*)<0. \]

For the example above,

\[ f(x)=2x-x^2, \]

so

\[ f''(x)=-2<0. \]

Therefore \(x^*=1\) is a local maximum.

Concavity

Concavity gives a stronger and cleaner way to verify a maximum.

If \(f\) is concave, then any point satisfying the first-order condition is a global maximum. If \(f\) is strictly concave, the maximum is unique.

A function is concave when it bends downward. Formally, \(f\) is concave if for all \(x,y\) and all \(\alpha \in [0,1]\),

\[ f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y) \geq \alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(y). \]

The intuition is that the value of a mixture is at least as high as the mixture of values.

Concavity is useful in economics because it often represents diminishing marginal returns.

Optimization and best responses

A best response in game theory is an optimization problem.

Given the strategies of other players, \(s_{-i}\), player \(i\) chooses a strategy \(s_i\) that maximizes their payoff:

\[ BR_i(s_{-i}) \in \arg\max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i,s_{-i}). \]

This says:

Hold everyone else’s strategies fixed, then choose the best available response.

A Nash equilibrium is a profile of strategies where everyone is already choosing a best response:

\[ s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for every player } i. \]

So optimization is not separate from equilibrium analysis. It is the local decision problem inside equilibrium reasoning.

Example: best response in a Cournot game

Suppose two firms choose quantities \(q_1\) and \(q_2\). Market price is

\[ P = a - b(q_1+q_2), \]

and firm \(i\) has constant marginal cost \(c\).

Firm 1’s profit is

\[ \pi_1(q_1,q_2) = (a-b(q_1+q_2))q_1-cq_1. \]

Simplify:

\[ \pi_1(q_1,q_2) = (a-c)q_1-bq_1^2-bq_1q_2. \]

Firm 1 chooses \(q_1\) taking \(q_2\) as given:

\[ \max_{q_1 \geq 0} (a-c)q_1-bq_1^2-bq_1q_2. \]

The first-order condition is

\[ \frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q_1} = (a-c)-2bq_1-bq_2=0. \]

Solving for \(q_1\) gives firm 1’s best response:

\[ q_1 = \frac{a-c-bq_2}{2b}. \]

With the non-negativity constraint, the best response is

\[ BR_1(q_2) = \max\left\{0,\frac{a-c-bq_2}{2b}\right\}. \]

This example shows why optimization is the foundation of best-response analysis.

Constrained optimization

Many choices are constrained. Consumers face budgets, firms face capacity, players face strategy restrictions, and institutions face feasibility constraints.

A constrained optimization problem can be written as

\[ \max_x f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g(x)\leq 0. \]

The objective says what the decision-maker wants.
The constraint says what the decision-maker is allowed to do.

Lagrangian method

For an equality-constrained problem,

\[ \max_x f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad h(x)=0, \]

the Lagrangian is

\[ \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda)=f(x)-\lambda h(x). \]

The first-order conditions are

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x}=0 \]

and

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \lambda}=0. \]

The multiplier \(\lambda\) measures the shadow value of relaxing the constraint.

In economic terms, a shadow value tells us how much the objective would improve if the constraint became slightly less restrictive.

KKT conditions

For inequality constraints, use the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions.

Consider

\[ \max_x f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g_j(x)\leq 0 \quad \text{for } j=1,\ldots,m. \]

The Lagrangian is

\[ \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda) = f(x)-\sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_j g_j(x), \qquad \lambda_j \geq 0. \]

The KKT conditions are:

  1. Stationarity

\[ \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x^*,\lambda^*)=0. \]

  1. Primal feasibility

\[ g_j(x^*)\leq 0 \quad \text{for all } j. \]

  1. Dual feasibility

\[ \lambda_j^* \geq 0 \quad \text{for all } j. \]

  1. Complementary slackness

\[ \lambda_j^* g_j(x^*)=0 \quad \text{for all } j. \]

The intuition is simple:

  • if a constraint does not bind, its multiplier is zero;
  • if a constraint binds, its multiplier may be positive.

Binding and non-binding constraints

A constraint is binding if it holds with equality at the optimum.

For example, if the constraint is

\[ x \leq 10, \]

then it binds when

\[ x^*=10. \]

A constraint is non-binding if it does not restrict the optimum.

In KKT terms:

\[ \lambda_j^* g_j(x^*)=0. \]

This means either:

\[ \lambda_j^*=0, \]

or

\[ g_j(x^*)=0. \]

So a constraint either has no shadow value, or it binds.

Example: constrained optimization

Maximize

\[ f(x)=2x-x^2 \]

subject to

\[ x\geq 0. \]

Write the constraint as

\[ g(x)=-x\leq 0. \]

The Lagrangian is

\[ \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda) = 2x-x^2-\lambda(-x) = 2x-x^2+\lambda x. \]

The KKT conditions are:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x} = 2-2x+\lambda=0, \]

\[ x\geq 0, \]

\[ \lambda \geq 0, \]

and

\[ \lambda x=0. \]

From stationarity,

\[ 2-2x+\lambda=0. \]

So

\[ x=1+\frac{\lambda}{2}. \]

If \(x>0\), then complementary slackness implies

\[ \lambda=0. \]

Therefore

\[ x^*=1. \]

This is feasible, so the solution is

\[ x^*=1. \]

The constraint does not bind.

Boundary example

Now consider

\[ \max_{x\geq 0} -x. \]

The unconstrained objective is decreasing, so the best feasible choice is

\[ x^*=0. \]

Here the constraint binds. The decision-maker would like to choose a lower value of \(x\), but the feasible set does not allow it.

This kind of example is important because not every optimum is found by setting the derivative equal to zero.

Comparative statics

Comparative statics asks how the solution changes when a parameter changes.

Suppose the optimal choice is

\[ x^*(a). \]

A comparative static asks:

\[ \frac{d x^*(a)}{d a}. \]

In words:

When the parameter \(a\) changes, how does the optimal choice change?

In game theory, comparative statics often ask how equilibrium strategies change when costs, benefits, information, discount factors, or enforcement probabilities change.

For example:

  • How does a firm’s quantity change when marginal cost rises?
  • How does crime change when the probability of detection rises?
  • How does cooperation change when players become more patient?
  • How does communication change when preferences become more aligned?

Optimization versus equilibrium

Optimization is individual. Equilibrium is collective.

An individual optimization problem asks:

\[ \max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i,s_{-i}). \]

An equilibrium asks whether every player is solving their own optimization problem at the same time:

\[ s_i^* \in \arg\max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i,s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for every } i. \]

This is the bridge from calculus to game theory.

Common mistakes

1. Confusing max and argmax

The maximum is the value of the objective. The argmax is the choice that achieves it.

2. Ignoring the feasible set

An unconstrained optimum may be infeasible. Always check constraints.

3. Applying FOC to boundary solutions

The first-order condition is necessary for interior optima, not for all optima.

4. Forgetting the second-order condition

A stationary point may be a maximum, minimum, or saddle point.

5. Treating best responses as fixed numbers

Best responses usually depend on other players’ strategies. A best response is often a function, not just a single value.

6. Forgetting interpretation

Solving the math is only part of the work. The final step is explaining what the result means in the model.

Checklist for solving an optimization problem

Use this checklist before moving to equilibrium analysis:

  1. What is the choice variable?
  2. What is the objective function?
  3. What is the feasible set?
  4. Is the problem constrained or unconstrained?
  5. Is the solution interior or on the boundary?
  6. Can FOC be used?
  7. Does SOC or concavity confirm a maximum?
  8. What does the solution mean economically?
  9. How does the solution change when parameters change?
  10. If this is a game, how does this become a best response?
