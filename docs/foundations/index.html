Foundations

The foundations section collects the mathematical and conceptual tools used across the site. These pages are not separate from the substantive notes. They are the background machinery behind game theory, criminology models, psychology overlaps, and rationality under uncertainty.

The aim is practical: each foundation page should help explain how to read, build, or interpret a model.

Map of the foundations section

The core idea is that formal models usually combine several foundations at once. A Bayesian game, for example, uses probability for beliefs, optimization for best responses, and fixed-point reasoning for equilibrium.

Suggested reading order

If you are reading the site from the beginning, use this order:

  1. Optimization
  2. Probability
  3. Dynamic reasoning
  4. Fixed points
  5. Convexity
  6. Linear algebra

This order works because optimization and probability appear almost everywhere. Dynamic reasoning becomes important once timing matters. Fixed points, convexity, and linear algebra become more important when the models become more abstract.

Foundations for game theory

Game theory uses all of these tools.

Foundation Use in game theory
Optimization Best responses and payoff maximization
Probability Mixed strategies, beliefs, types, and signals
Dynamic reasoning Sequential moves, credibility, and backward induction
Fixed points Existence of equilibrium
Convexity Strategy spaces, mixtures, and well-behaved optimization
Linear algebra Mixed strategies, systems of equations, and matrix representation

A Nash equilibrium can be understood as a fixed point of best-response reasoning. A Bayesian equilibrium combines optimization with beliefs. A dynamic equilibrium combines optimization with histories and continuation values.

Foundations for criminology

The same tools also appear in criminology.

Foundation Use in criminology
Optimization Choice under incentives, sanctions, and opportunity
Probability Risk, detection, reporting, and uncertainty
Dynamic reasoning deterrence, retaliation, reputation, and life-course processes
Fixed points social norms and stable patterns of behaviour
Convexity trade-offs in policy design and resource allocation
Linear algebra networks, transitions, and population-level systems

The goal is not to reduce crime to equations. The goal is to make assumptions visible and to understand how mechanisms generate predictions.

Foundations for psychology

Psychology overlaps with these foundations when studying belief formation, bounded reasoning, learning, preference, emotion, and social cognition.

For example:

  • probability helps describe belief updating;
  • optimization helps define rational benchmarks;
  • dynamic reasoning helps analyse planning and self-control;
  • fixed points help describe stable expectations or norms;
  • convexity helps formalise trade-offs;
  • linear algebra helps represent cognitive or social systems.

This is where the site’s broader theme appears: rationality is not only a property of isolated individuals. It is shaped by uncertainty, interaction, time, and institutions.

How to use the foundation pages

Each foundation page should be read with three questions:

  1. What problem does this tool solve?
  2. Where does it appear in a model?
  3. What mistake does it help prevent?

For example, optimization prevents vague claims about “choosing well.” Probability prevents vague claims about “uncertainty.” Dynamic reasoning prevents vague claims about “future threats.” Fixed points prevent vague claims about “stable outcomes.”

Common mistakes

1. Treating foundations as detached mathematics

The tools are useful because they organise substantive arguments. They should be connected to models and examples.

2. Memorising formulas without knowing the role

A formula matters because it does something in an argument: maximizes, updates, compares, verifies, or predicts.

3. Skipping assumptions

Most formal results depend on assumptions such as differentiability, concavity, complete information, common knowledge, or finite action spaces.

4. Ignoring interpretation

A mathematical solution is not the end of the analysis. The final step is to explain what the result means.
