Dynamic Reasoning

Dynamic reasoning studies decisions that unfold over time. In a static problem, a player chooses once. In a dynamic problem, players act after histories, observe earlier moves, form expectations about later moves, and ask whether threats or promises are credible.

This page introduces the logic behind backward induction, sequential rationality, credibility, continuation payoffs, and dynamic equilibrium.

Why dynamic reasoning matters

Timing changes strategic behaviour.

In a simultaneous game, each player chooses without observing the current action of the other. In a dynamic game, one player may observe an earlier move and respond. This creates new strategic possibilities:

  • threats;
  • promises;
  • commitment;
  • reputation;
  • punishment;
  • learning;
  • signalling;
  • waiting;
  • delay.

A dynamic game is not just a static game with time added. The order of moves can change the result.

Dynamic reasoning therefore often works backwards: to decide what should happen now, we first ask what would happen later.

Histories and continuation

A history is a sequence of actions that has occurred so far.

For example, in a simple two-stage game:

  1. Player 1 chooses \(A\) or \(B\).
  2. Player 2 observes Player 1’s action.
  3. Player 2 chooses \(L\) or \(R\).

After Player 1 chooses \(A\), the history is

\[ h = A. \]

After Player 1 chooses \(B\), the history is

\[ h = B. \]

Player 2 may choose differently after different histories. This is why a strategy in a dynamic game must specify what a player would do after every relevant history.

Strategies in dynamic games

In a static game, a strategy can often be thought of as one action.

In a dynamic game, a strategy is a complete contingent plan. It says what the player will do at every decision point where they may be called to move.

For example, if Player 2 moves after either \(A\) or \(B\), then a strategy for Player 2 must specify:

\[ s_2(A) \]

and

\[ s_2(B). \]

A complete strategy might be:

\[ s_2 = (L \text{ after } A,\ R \text{ after } B). \]

This matters because equilibrium requires optimality not only along the path that actually occurs, but also after possible histories that might not occur.

The logic of backward induction

Backward induction solves a finite dynamic game by starting from the final decision nodes and moving backward to the beginning.

The key idea is simple:

To know what a player should do now, first determine what later players would rationally do.

Backward induction is therefore reasoning from the future back to the present.

A small example

Suppose Player 1 chooses whether to Enter or Stay Out.

If Player 1 stays out, the game ends. If Player 1 enters, Player 2 chooses whether to Fight or Accommodate.

The game has this structure:

To solve this game, do not start by asking whether Player 1 should enter. Start at Player 2’s decision node.

If Player 2 would prefer to accommodate rather than fight after entry, then Player 1 anticipates accommodation. Player 1’s entry decision should be evaluated against that expected response.

If Player 2 would prefer to fight after entry, then Player 1 anticipates fighting. Player 1’s entry decision may change.

The first move depends on the credible second move.

Credibility

A threat is credible if the player would actually want to carry it out when the time comes.

A promise is credible if the player would actually want to honour it when the time comes.

In dynamic games, announcements alone are not enough. What matters is whether the announced behaviour is sequentially optimal.

This is one of the most important lessons of dynamic reasoning: future behaviour must be optimal when the future arrives.

Sequential rationality

A strategy is sequentially rational if it prescribes optimal behaviour at every decision point, given the player’s beliefs and continuation payoffs.

This is stronger than simply being optimal at the start of the game.

For example, a player may announce:

“If you enter, I will fight.”

But if fighting is costly and accommodation is better after entry occurs, then fighting is not sequentially rational. The threat is not credible.

Sequential rationality asks:

At each point in the game, given the history so far, is this action optimal from that point onward?

Subgames

A subgame is a part of a dynamic game that begins at a decision node and includes all later actions and payoffs from that node onward.

A strategy profile is a subgame perfect equilibrium if it induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame.

The intuition is:

A dynamic equilibrium should remain rational after every possible history.

This rules out equilibria supported by non-credible threats.

Backward induction and subgame perfection

In finite games with perfect information, backward induction produces a subgame perfect equilibrium.

The relationship is:

Backward induction is a procedure.
Subgame perfection is a solution concept.

They are related, but not identical.

Perfect information and imperfect information

A game has perfect information if every player observes all previous actions before choosing.

A game has imperfect information if some player does not observe some earlier action, or if decision nodes are grouped into information sets.

Perfect-information games can often be solved by backward induction. Imperfect-information games may require beliefs.

When players do not know exactly where they are in the game, dynamic reasoning becomes belief-based.

Continuation payoffs

A continuation payoff is the payoff a player expects from a point in the game onward.

Dynamic reasoning often compares current actions by asking how they change future continuation payoffs.

For example, in a repeated relationship, cheating today may give a current benefit but reduce future continuation value.

The basic comparison is:

\[ \text{current payoff} + \text{future continuation payoff}. \]

If a player discounts the future by \(\delta \in (0,1)\), then the value of a stream of future payoffs may be written as

\[ U = u_0 + \delta u_1 + \delta^2 u_2 + \cdots. \]

The discount factor \(\delta\) measures patience. A higher \(\delta\) means the player cares more about future payoffs.

Dynamic incentives

A dynamic incentive exists when current behaviour is shaped by future consequences.

This logic appears in:

  • repeated games;
  • reputation models;
  • relational contracts;
  • crime and punishment;
  • trust and betrayal;
  • cooperation and retaliation;
  • political promises;
  • institutional enforcement.

The strategic force is the same: future responses can discipline current behaviour.

Repeated games

Repeated games are dynamic games where the same stage game is played more than once.

Repetition can support cooperation because players can reward or punish one another across time.

For example, suppose cooperation gives long-run benefits but defection gives a short-run gain. Cooperation may be sustained if the future loss from punishment is larger than the current gain from defection.

A typical incentive condition has the form:

\[ \text{gain from deviation} \leq \text{future loss from punishment}. \]

This is one of the central ideas behind repeated-game cooperation.

Commitment

Commitment changes dynamic reasoning by changing what future actions are available or attractive.

A player has commitment power if they can bind themselves to a future action.

Without commitment, a promise may fail because the player will later want to break it.

With commitment, the same promise may become credible.

Commitment is important in contracts, bargaining, monetary policy, deterrence, and institutional design.

Dynamic reasoning in Bayesian settings

When dynamic games include incomplete information, players must reason about both future actions and beliefs.

For example, if a sender sends a message, the receiver may update beliefs about the sender’s type. The receiver then chooses a response. The sender anticipates this response when choosing the message.

Common mistakes

1. Solving from the beginning

In finite perfect-information games, solve from the end first. The first move depends on later credible responses.

2. Confusing actions and strategies

In dynamic games, a strategy is a complete plan for every relevant decision point.

3. Ignoring off-path behaviour

Equilibrium may require specifying what players would do after histories that do not occur on the equilibrium path.

4. Treating threats as automatically credible

A threat affects behaviour only if it is optimal to carry out when the time comes.

5. Forgetting beliefs

In dynamic games with incomplete information, players must update beliefs after observations.

6. Confusing backward induction with equilibrium

Backward induction is a method. Subgame perfect equilibrium is a solution concept.

Checklist for dynamic games

When analysing a dynamic game, ask:

  1. Who moves first?
  2. Who moves later?
  3. What does each player observe before choosing?
  4. What are the possible histories?
  5. What is each player’s complete strategy?
  6. What happens at the final decision nodes?
  7. Which threats or promises are credible?
  8. Are actions optimal in every subgame?
  9. Are beliefs needed?
  10. What is the equilibrium path?
