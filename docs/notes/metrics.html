Metrics

Metrics are tools for making comparisons precise. They turn concepts into quantities, but they also introduce assumptions. A metric is useful only when we understand what it measures, what it leaves out, and how it can be misread.

This page collects basic measurement ideas used across the site.

Why metrics matter

Many arguments depend on measurement.

In game theory, we compare payoffs, beliefs, welfare, strategies, and equilibrium outcomes.

In criminology, we compare crime counts, rates, reporting, risk, harm, institutional outcomes, and policy effects.

In psychology, we compare behaviour, beliefs, symptoms, responses, and decision patterns.

The danger is that the metric may look precise while the underlying concept remains unclear.

Count and rate

A count records the number of events.

A rate normalizes the count by a relevant denominator.

For example:

\[ \text{Crime rate} = \frac{\text{Number of crimes}}{\text{Population}} \times 100{,}000. \]

Counts are useful for scale. Rates are useful for comparison.

If City A has more crimes than City B, it may simply have a larger population. A rate adjusts for this.

Choosing the denominator

A rate depends on the denominator.

Possible denominators include:

  • population;
  • households;
  • firms;
  • users;
  • opportunities;
  • time exposed;
  • number of transactions;
  • number of police stops;
  • number of cases;
  • number of people at risk.

Different denominators answer different questions.

For example, burglary per resident and burglary per household are not the same measure.

Stock and flow

A stock is measured at a point in time.
A flow is measured over an interval.

Examples:

Concept Stock Flow
prison population people in prison on a date admissions or releases per year
unemployment people unemployed now people entering unemployment
cases pending cases new filings
beliefs current probability belief belief update after signal

Stock-flow confusion is common. A system can have a high stock because inflow is high, outflow is low, or duration is long.

Prevalence and incidence

Prevalence asks how widespread something is in a population.

Incidence asks how many new events occur over a period.

For example:

prevalence: share of people victimized this year
incidence: number of victimization events this year

If the same person is victimized multiple times, incidence may be high even when prevalence is lower.

Risk and probability

A risk is often represented as a probability.

For example:

\[ \Pr(\text{victimization}) \]

or

\[ \Pr(\text{detection} \mid \text{offending}). \]

Conditional probabilities are especially important.

For example:

\[ \Pr(\text{recorded} \mid \text{reported}) \]

is different from

\[ \Pr(\text{reported} \mid \text{crime occurred}). \]

Each transition has its own probability.

Expected value

Expected value is a probability-weighted average.

If outcome \(x_k\) occurs with probability \(p_k\), then

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_k p_k x_k. \]

Expected value is central to decision theory, deterrence, and game theory.

For example, expected punishment can be written as:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{punishment}] = \Pr(\text{caught}) \times \text{sanction}. \]

This simple expression is useful, but incomplete. It assumes the actor perceives the probability and sanction, and that they are responsive to expected future costs.

Accuracy, precision, and bias

A metric can fail in different ways.

Term Meaning
Accuracy closeness to the true value
Precision consistency or narrowness of measurement
Bias systematic error
Noise random variation
Validity whether the measure captures the intended concept
Reliability whether measurement is consistent

A measure can be precise but biased. It can be reliable but invalid.

For example, recorded crime can be measured consistently but still fail to capture true crime if reporting is low.

Proxy measures

A proxy is a measurable variable used to approximate a harder-to-measure concept.

Examples:

Concept Possible proxy
trust in police survey response or reporting behaviour
offending arrest, conviction, self-report, victimization data
social disorder calls for service, complaints, observation
deterrence perceived detection risk
harm injury, loss, trauma, cost
ability test score or qualification

A proxy should never be treated as identical to the concept.

Latent concepts

Some concepts are not directly observed. These are latent concepts.

Examples:

  • trust;
  • fear;
  • self-control;
  • legitimacy;
  • belief;
  • intention;
  • risk perception;
  • social cohesion.

Latent concepts require careful measurement. A single proxy may capture only one dimension.

Using several measures can improve interpretation.

Belief accuracy

In models of rationality and strategic interaction, we may ask whether beliefs are accurate.

Suppose the true probability of an event is \(p\), while a person believes it is \(\hat p\).

A simple error measure is:

\[ |\hat p - p|. \]

Belief accuracy matters in:

  • Bayesian updating;
  • deterrence;
  • risk perception;
  • trust;
  • reputation;
  • learning;
  • prediction.

A person may behave “rationally” given their belief, even if the belief itself is inaccurate.

Payoff comparison

Game theory often compares payoffs across actions.

Player \(i\) prefers action \(a\) over action \(b\) when

\[ u_i(a) > u_i(b). \]

In strategic settings, payoffs depend on what others do:

\[ u_i(a_i,a_{-i}). \]

Payoff comparisons become best-response comparisons:

\[ a_i^* \in \arg\max_{a_i} u_i(a_i,a_{-i}). \]

The metric here is not necessarily money. It is an ordering of outcomes.

Welfare comparison

Welfare comparisons ask whether one outcome is socially better than another.

A simple utilitarian welfare function is:

\[ W = \sum_i u_i. \]

But welfare comparison can be difficult because:

  • utility may not be comparable across people;
  • distribution may matter;
  • rights and fairness may matter;
  • harm may be multidimensional;
  • some costs are hidden or delayed.

A model may identify an efficient outcome, but policy interpretation still requires ethical judgement.

Rates over time

When comparing rates over time, ask:

  1. Did the numerator change?
  2. Did the denominator change?
  3. Did measurement practice change?
  4. Did reporting change?
  5. Did the population at risk change?
  6. Did the category definition change?

A trend is not automatically an explanation.

Common mistakes

1. Comparing counts when rates are needed

A larger place may have more events simply because it has more people or opportunities.

2. Using the wrong denominator

The denominator should match the population at risk.

3. Treating proxy as concept

A proxy is evidence about a concept, not the concept itself.

4. Ignoring measurement process

Metrics are produced through institutions, surveys, instruments, or coding rules.

5. Confusing prediction with explanation

A variable may predict an outcome without causing it.

6. Ignoring uncertainty

Point estimates should be interpreted with uncertainty, variation, and possible error in mind.

Checklist for using a metric

  1. What concept is being measured?
  2. What is the exact metric?
  3. What is the numerator?
  4. What is the denominator?
  5. What is the unit of analysis?
  6. Is it a count, rate, stock, flow, prevalence, or incidence?
  7. Is the measure a direct measure or proxy?
  8. What measurement process produced it?
  9. What bias or missingness may exist?
  10. What comparison is being made?
  11. What interpretation is justified?
  12. What interpretation would be too strong?
