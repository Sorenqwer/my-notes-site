Nash Equilibrium

Nash equilibrium is the basic solution concept of non-cooperative game theory. It describes a strategy profile in which every player is choosing a best response to the strategies of the others.

The central idea is:

No player can profitably deviate alone.

The definition

Let

\[ G=\langle N,(S_i)_{i\in N},(u_i)_{i\in N}\rangle \]

be a normal-form game.

A strategy profile

\[ s^*=(s_1^*,\ldots,s_n^*) \]

is a Nash equilibrium if, for every player \(i\),

\[ u_i(s_i^*,s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i,s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for all } s_i\in S_i. \]

This means that \(s_i^*\) solves player \(i\)’s optimization problem given the strategies of the other players.

Equivalently,

\[ s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for every } i. \]

Intuition

A Nash equilibrium is a state of mutual best response.

The equilibrium is stable in a specific sense: if everyone expects the equilibrium strategies, no single player wants to change their own strategy.

What Nash equilibrium does and does not say

Nash equilibrium says:

  • each player is individually optimizing;
  • each player takes others’ strategies as given;
  • no player has a profitable unilateral deviation.

It does not say:

  • the outcome is fair;
  • the outcome is socially efficient;
  • the outcome is morally good;
  • players are happy;
  • the equilibrium is unique;
  • players will necessarily learn their way to it.

This distinction is essential.

Best-response method

To find a Nash equilibrium, find mutual best responses.

In a payoff matrix, a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium is a cell where each player’s chosen strategy is a best response to the other player’s chosen strategy.

Example: coordination game

Consider this game:

Player 2: L Player 2: R
Player 1: U \((2,2)\) \((0,0)\)
Player 1: D \((0,0)\) \((1,1)\)

If Player 2 chooses \(L\), Player 1 prefers \(U\).

If Player 2 chooses \(R\), Player 1 prefers \(D\).

If Player 1 chooses \(U\), Player 2 prefers \(L\).

If Player 1 chooses \(D\), Player 2 prefers \(R\).

Therefore, there are two Nash equilibria:

\[ (U,L) \]

and

\[ (D,R). \]

This shows that Nash equilibrium may not be unique.

Example: prisoners’ dilemma

In a prisoners’ dilemma, each player has an individual incentive to defect, even though both would be better off if both cooperated.

The usual ranking is:

\[ T > R > P > S, \]

where:

Symbol Meaning
\(T\) temptation payoff from defecting while the other cooperates
\(R\) reward from mutual cooperation
\(P\) punishment from mutual defection
\(S\) sucker payoff from cooperating while the other defects

The Nash equilibrium is mutual defection.

This outcome is stable because no player wants to cooperate unilaterally. But it is inefficient because both players would prefer mutual cooperation.

Nash equilibrium and inefficiency

A Nash equilibrium can be inefficient.

This is one of the main lessons of game theory. Individual rationality does not automatically produce collective welfare.

Examples include:

  • prisoners’ dilemma;
  • arms races;
  • overuse of common resources;
  • price competition;

Mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium may involve mixed strategies.

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies.

In a mixed-strategy equilibrium, each player must be willing to randomize among the pure strategies used with positive probability.

This means the player must be indifferent among those pure strategies.

For example, if Player 1 mixes between \(U\) and \(D\), then

\[ U_1(U,\sigma_2)=U_1(D,\sigma_2). \]

If one pure strategy gave a strictly higher expected payoff, Player 1 would not randomize.

Indifference principle

The indifference principle is often used to solve mixed equilibria.

This can feel strange at first. When solving for Player 2’s mixing probability, we often use Player 1’s indifference condition. Player 2 randomizes in a way that makes Player 1 willing to mix.

Nash equilibrium as a fixed point

Nash equilibrium can be understood as a fixed point of the best-response correspondence.

Let \(BR(s)\) be the set of best-response profiles to \(s\). A Nash equilibrium satisfies

\[ s^* \in BR(s^*). \]

This means that if everyone best responds to \(s^*\), the resulting strategy profile can still be \(s^*\).

This connection explains why fixed-point theorems are used to prove equilibrium existence.

Existence

Every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

This does not mean every finite game has a pure-strategy equilibrium.

For example, matching pennies has no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, but it has a mixed-strategy equilibrium.

The existence result relies on allowing players to randomize.

Multiple equilibria

A game may have more than one Nash equilibrium.

Multiple equilibria create a selection problem:

If several outcomes are stable, which one should we expect?

Possible selection forces include:

  • focal points;
  • history;
  • communication;
  • institutions;
  • conventions;
  • risk dominance;
  • payoff dominance;
  • learning;
  • equilibrium refinements.

Coordination games often have multiple equilibria.

Nash equilibrium in dynamic games

Nash equilibrium can be applied to dynamic games by treating strategies as complete contingent plans.

However, Nash equilibrium alone may allow non-credible threats.

This is why dynamic games often use stronger concepts such as subgame perfect equilibrium.

Example:

A player threatens to punish later, but when later arrives, punishment is costly and irrational.

Nash equilibrium may not rule this out unless the threat is tested inside the relevant subgame.

Common mistakes

1. Thinking Nash equilibrium means the best outcome

Nash equilibrium is about stability, not welfare.

2. Forgetting unilateral deviation

Nash equilibrium checks whether one player can improve by deviating alone, holding others fixed.

3. Ignoring mixed strategies

A game with no pure equilibrium may still have a mixed equilibrium.

4. Confusing best response with dominant strategy

A best response depends on what others do. A dominant strategy is best no matter what others do.

5. Treating multiple equilibria as a contradiction

Multiple equilibria mean several outcomes are strategically stable.

6. Using Nash equilibrium when timing matters

For dynamic games, check whether subgame perfection or another refinement is needed.

Checklist for finding Nash equilibrium

  1. Identify players.
  2. List strategies.
  3. Write payoffs.
  4. For each opponent strategy, find each player’s best response.
  5. Mark mutual best responses.
  6. Check if any player can profitably deviate alone.
  7. If no pure equilibrium exists, check mixed strategies.
  8. If multiple equilibria exist, interpret the selection problem.
  9. Check whether the equilibrium is efficient.
  10. If the game is dynamic, ask whether the equilibrium is credible.
