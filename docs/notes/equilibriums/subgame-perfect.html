Subgame Perfect Equilibrium

Subgame perfect equilibrium is a refinement of Nash equilibrium for dynamic games. It rules out equilibria that rely on non-credible threats or promises.

The central idea is:

behaviour should be optimal after every possible history, not only at the beginning of the game.

Why Nash equilibrium is not enough

Nash equilibrium checks whether players want to deviate from their complete strategies.

In dynamic games, this can allow threats that would not actually be carried out if the relevant history occurred.

Subgame perfection adds credibility.

Definition

A strategy profile is a subgame perfect equilibrium if it induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame.

In words:

after every history that begins a subgame, the continuation strategies must form a Nash equilibrium of that subgame.

This means players must behave optimally not only along the equilibrium path, but also after possible deviations.

Subgames

A subgame is a part of an extensive-form game that:

  1. begins at a decision node;
  2. includes all future nodes and actions from that node;
  3. does not cut across an information set.

A subgame is like a smaller game inside the larger game.

Backward induction

In finite games with perfect information, backward induction finds a subgame perfect equilibrium.

Backward induction is the procedure. Subgame perfect equilibrium is the solution concept.

Non-credible threat

The incumbent may want to threaten:

If you enter, I will fight.

But after entry occurs, fighting gives the incumbent a lower payoff. Therefore the threat is not credible.

Subgame perfection removes equilibria supported by this kind of threat.

Strategy profiles and paths

In dynamic games, an equilibrium strategy profile specifies:

  1. what happens on the equilibrium path;
  2. what would happen after off-path histories.

The equilibrium path is the sequence of actions that actually occurs if everyone follows the equilibrium strategies.

Off-path behaviour describes what players would do after deviations.

Subgame perfection requires off-path behaviour to be optimal in every subgame.

Sequential rationality

Subgame perfection is closely related to sequential rationality.

Sequential rationality means that at every point where a player acts, the prescribed action is optimal given the continuation game.

In perfect-information games, subgame perfection captures this idea well.

In imperfect-information games, we often need belief-based refinements such as perfect Bayesian equilibrium or sequential equilibrium.

SPE versus Nash equilibrium

Concept Requirement Problem addressed
Nash equilibrium no profitable unilateral deviation from complete strategy strategic stability
Subgame perfect equilibrium Nash equilibrium in every subgame credibility
Perfect Bayesian equilibrium sequential rationality plus beliefs dynamic incomplete information

Every subgame perfect equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium. But not every Nash equilibrium is subgame perfect.

Finite-horizon repeated games

Subgame perfection explains unraveling in finitely repeated prisoners’ dilemma games.

If the final period is known, players defect in the final period. Knowing that, they defect in the previous period, and so on.

This is a classic application of backward induction.

One-deviation principle

In many dynamic games, the one-deviation principle can simplify checking subgame perfection.

The idea is:

a strategy profile is subgame perfect if no player can gain by deviating once and then returning to the strategy.

This works under particular conditions, especially in many finite or discounted dynamic games.

It is useful because checking every possible deviation can be hard.

Common mistakes

1. Thinking SPE is only about the equilibrium path

SPE also requires optimal behaviour after off-path histories.

2. Treating threats as credible because they are announced

A threat is credible only if it is optimal to carry out.

3. Confusing backward induction with SPE

Backward induction is a method; SPE is a solution concept.

4. Applying subgame perfection where there are no proper subgames

In some imperfect-information games, subgame perfection may add little beyond Nash equilibrium.

5. Cutting through information sets

A subgame cannot begin in the middle of an information set.

Checklist

When checking subgame perfection, ask:

  1. What is the extensive-form game?
  2. What are the proper subgames?
  3. What strategy profile is proposed?
  4. Does the profile induce a Nash equilibrium in the whole game?
  5. Does it induce a Nash equilibrium in every subgame?
  6. Are threats credible?
  7. Are promises credible?
  8. What is the equilibrium path?
  9. What happens off path?
  10. Would backward induction give the same result in a finite perfect-information game?
