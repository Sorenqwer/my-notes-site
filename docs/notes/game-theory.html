Game Theory Notes

Game theory studies situations in which outcomes depend not only on one person’s choice, but also on the choices, beliefs, and incentives of others. It is useful whenever behaviour is strategic: each player asks not only “What should I do?”, but also “What will others do, what do they believe, and how will they respond?”

The aim of these notes is not to turn every social situation into a mathematical puzzle. It is to build a disciplined language for thinking about interdependence: conflict, cooperation, credibility, information, bargaining, punishment, trust, and reputation.

The basic idea

A decision problem becomes a game when one person’s payoff depends on another person’s action. In an ordinary optimization problem, the decision-maker chooses the best action against a fixed environment. In a game, the “environment” also thinks, chooses, reacts, and sometimes tries to anticipate you.

This is why game theory is central to economics, politics, law, criminology, psychology, and everyday social life. Prices, contracts, promises, threats, negotiations, norms, crime, trust, and communication often depend on strategic interaction.

Basic ingredients of a game

A game normally specifies four objects:

  1. Players: the decision-makers.
  2. Strategies: the complete plans available to each player.
  3. Information: what each player knows when they choose.
  4. Payoffs: how each player ranks the possible outcomes.

A simple normal-form game can be written as

\[ G = \langle N, (S_i)_{i \in N}, (u_i)_{i \in N} \rangle, \]

where \(N\) is the set of players, \(S_i\) is player \(i\)’s strategy set, and \(u_i\) is player \(i\)’s payoff function.

For a strategy profile \(s=(s_1,\ldots,s_n)\), player \(i\)’s payoff is written as

\[ u_i(s_i,s_{-i}), \]

where \(s_i\) is player \(i\)’s own strategy and \(s_{-i}\) denotes the strategies chosen by everyone else.

The modelling workflow

A useful way to study a game is to move from primitives to solution concept in a fixed order.

This workflow prevents a common mistake: solving for an equilibrium before the game itself has been clearly defined.

Best responses

The most basic object in game theory is the best response. Given the strategies of others, player \(i\) chooses a strategy that maximizes their payoff:

\[ BR_i(s_{-i}) \in \arg\max_{s_i \in S_i} u_i(s_i,s_{-i}). \]

The best response idea is simple but powerful. It says that a player’s action should be judged relative to what they believe others are doing.

For example, if a firm believes its competitor will set a low price, its best response may be to set a low price too. If it believes the competitor will set a high price, its best response may change. Strategic behaviour is therefore conditional behaviour.

Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile in which every player is choosing a best response to the strategies of the others. Formally, a strategy profile \(s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) is a Nash equilibrium if, for every player \(i\),

\[ u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for all } s_i \in S_i. \]

The key idea is mutual optimality: no player wants to deviate alone, taking the others’ strategies as given.

A Nash equilibrium does not necessarily mean that the outcome is socially good, fair, efficient, or morally desirable. It only means that no individual player has a unilateral incentive to change their strategy.

This distinction is important. A bad outcome can be stable if each player is individually responding optimally to the incentives they face.

Static games

In a static game, players choose actions without observing the current actions of others. The standard representation is the normal form, usually shown as a payoff matrix.

Static games are useful for studying:

  • price competition
  • public goods contribution
  • coordination problems
  • one-shot conflict
  • voting or participation decisions
  • inspection and enforcement problems

The main solution concept is usually Nash equilibrium.

See: Normal-form games.

Dynamic games

In a dynamic game, timing matters. Players move in sequence, observe earlier actions, or make plans that unfold over time.

Dynamic games introduce a new question: credibility.

A player may announce a threat or promise, but the announcement matters only if it is optimal to carry it out when the time comes. This is why dynamic games often require stronger solution concepts than ordinary Nash equilibrium.

The standard method in finite perfect-information games is backward induction: solve from the end of the game back to the beginning.

See: Extensive-form games and Dynamic reasoning.

Bayesian games

A Bayesian game introduces incomplete information. A player may not know another player’s type, payoff, cost, ability, preference, or intention.

Instead of knowing the whole game directly, players hold beliefs over possible types. These beliefs become part of strategic reasoning.

A Bayesian game usually includes:

  • a set of players
  • a set of possible types for each player
  • a prior belief over types
  • strategy sets conditional on type
  • payoffs that may depend on type

The central solution concept is Bayesian Nash equilibrium, where each type of each player chooses a best response given beliefs and other players’ type-contingent strategies.

See: Bayesian games, Bayesian Nash equilibrium, and Probability.

Communication games

Communication becomes strategic when one player has information and another player must decide whether to believe, ignore, or interpret the message.

There are several important cases:

Type Main idea
Cheap talk Messages are costless and non-binding
Signaling Messages or actions may be costly and informative
Screening The uninformed side designs choices to reveal information
Mechanism design The designer structures incentives to elicit behaviour or information

A central question is whether information can be transmitted when players’ interests are not perfectly aligned.

See: Cheap talk, Signaling, and Screening & mechanism design.

Equilibrium concepts

Different games require different equilibrium concepts. The choice of equilibrium concept depends on what the model needs to discipline.

Situation Typical solution concept What it disciplines
Simultaneous choice Nash equilibrium Mutual best responses
Sequential choice Subgame perfect equilibrium Credibility and sequential rationality
Incomplete information Bayesian Nash equilibrium Type-contingent optimality
Dynamic incomplete information Perfect Bayesian / sequential equilibrium Beliefs and sequential rationality
Robustness to small mistakes Trembling-hand perfect equilibrium Stability under perturbations
Correlated recommendations Correlated equilibrium Obedience to signals
Strategic reasoning without equilibrium Rationalizability Survival of best-response reasoning

See: Equilibrium concepts.

Efficiency and welfare

Equilibrium analysis is not the same as welfare analysis. A game may have an equilibrium that is inefficient.

A classic example is the prisoners’ dilemma. Each player has an individual incentive to defect, but mutual defection makes both players worse off than mutual cooperation.

This creates one of the central themes of game theory:

individually rational behaviour can produce collectively undesirable outcomes.

The tension between private incentives and collective welfare appears in public goods problems, arms races, price wars, overuse of common resources, and many forms of institutional failure.

Common modelling mistakes

1. Confusing actions and strategies

An action is a move at one decision point. A strategy is a complete plan. In dynamic games, a strategy must say what the player would do at every relevant information set, even after histories that do not occur in equilibrium.

2. Solving before defining the game

Before solving, specify players, strategies, information, timing, and payoffs. Many errors come from unclear primitives.

3. Treating equilibrium as moral approval

An equilibrium is a stability concept, not a claim that the outcome is good.

4. Ignoring off-path behaviour

In dynamic games, what players would do after unreached histories can affect whether threats and promises are credible.

5. Forgetting beliefs

In Bayesian and signaling games, actions cannot be understood without beliefs. What matters is not only what is true, but what players infer from what they observe.
