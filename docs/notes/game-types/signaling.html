Signaling

Signaling games study situations where an informed sender takes an action that may reveal private information to an uninformed receiver.

Unlike cheap talk, a signal often has a cost or direct payoff consequence. This cost can make the signal credible when different types face different costs or benefits.

Basic structure

A signaling game usually has this timing:

  1. Nature chooses the sender’s type.
  2. The sender observes their own type.
  3. The sender chooses a signal.
  4. The receiver observes the signal.
  5. The receiver forms a belief and chooses an action.
  6. Payoffs are realized.

Signaling is dynamic and involves incomplete information, so it usually requires belief-based equilibrium concepts such as perfect Bayesian equilibrium.

Why signaling matters

Many social and economic actions are partly informative.

Examples:

  • education may signal ability;
  • prices may signal quality;
  • costly effort may signal commitment;
  • apologies may signal remorse;
  • investment may signal confidence;
  • violence may signal toughness;
  • compliance systems may signal responsibility;
  • credentials may signal competence.

The key question is whether the signal separates types or merely creates appearance.

Types and signals

Let the sender’s type be:

\[ \theta \in \Theta. \]

Let the sender choose signal:

\[ s \in S. \]

The receiver observes \(s\) but not \(\theta\).

The receiver forms belief:

\[ \mu(\theta \mid s). \]

Then the receiver chooses action:

\[ a \in A. \]

A signaling equilibrium specifies:

  1. sender signal choices for each type;
  2. receiver beliefs after each signal;
  3. receiver actions after each signal.

Separating equilibrium

In a separating equilibrium, different types choose different signals.

Separating equilibria transmit information. The receiver can infer the sender’s type from the observed signal.

Pooling equilibrium

In a pooling equilibrium, different types choose the same signal.

Pooling equilibria transmit less information. The receiver’s belief after the pooled signal is based on the prior or equilibrium mixture.

Semi-separating equilibrium

In a semi-separating equilibrium, at least one type randomizes, or the signal only partly reveals type.

Semi-separating equilibria are common when incentives are close or when full separation is too costly.

Costly signals

A signal can be credible when it is less costly for one type than another.

For example, suppose education is easier for high-ability workers than low-ability workers. Then high education may signal high ability if low-ability workers find it too costly to imitate.

Incentive compatibility

A separating equilibrium requires incentive compatibility.

Each type must prefer the signal assigned to that type over imitating another type.

For high type \(H\) and low type \(L\), a separating equilibrium may require:

\[ U_H(s_H,a_H) \geq U_H(s_L,a_L), \]

and

\[ U_L(s_L,a_L) \geq U_L(s_H,a_H). \]

The first condition says the high type does not want to mimic the low type.
The second says the low type does not want to mimic the high type.

Receiver beliefs

Receiver beliefs must be consistent with observed signals on the equilibrium path.

If signal \(s_H\) is sent only by high type in equilibrium, then:

\[ \mu(H\mid s_H)=1. \]

If signal \(s_L\) is sent only by low type, then:

\[ \mu(L\mid s_L)=1. \]

If both types send the same signal, the receiver’s posterior is the prior conditional on pooling.

Off-path beliefs are more delicate and can affect equilibrium plausibility.

Education signaling example

In an education signaling model:

  • workers know their own ability;
  • firms do not observe ability;
  • workers choose education level;
  • firms observe education and offer wages;
  • education is less costly for high-ability workers.

Education may not directly increase productivity in a pure signaling model. It may work because it separates types.

Signaling versus human capital

Education can be interpreted in two ways.

Interpretation Main idea
Human capital education increases productivity
Signaling education reveals productivity or ability

Real education may do both. The model separates the mechanisms.

Signaling and social behaviour

Signaling is not only economic.

Examples:

Domain Signal Possible type being inferred
politics costly commitment ideological seriousness
crime retaliation toughness or willingness to punish
organizations compliance programme responsibility or legal caution
relationships apology remorse or sincerity
markets warranty product quality
academia credential ability or training

The key question is always whether the signal is incentive-compatible.

Cheap talk versus signaling

Feature Cheap talk Signaling
Cost costless often costly
Direct payoff effect none may directly affect payoff
Credibility source preference alignment differential cost or incentive
Main issue can messages be informative? can actions reveal type?

Cheap talk can reveal information without cost if preferences are aligned. Signaling can reveal information even with conflict if imitation is too costly.

Common mistakes

1. Treating every observable action as a signal

An action is a signal only if it changes beliefs about hidden information.

2. Ignoring signal cost

If imitation is costless, separation may fail.

3. Forgetting receiver beliefs

The receiver’s action depends on posterior beliefs.

4. Assuming separating equilibrium always exists

Separation requires incentive compatibility.

5. Confusing signaling with productivity

A signal may reveal ability without causing ability.

6. Ignoring off-path beliefs

Unexpected signals can support or undermine equilibria.

Checklist

When analysing a signaling game, ask:

  1. What is the sender’s private type?
  2. What signal can the sender choose?
  3. Does the signal have a cost?
  4. Does the cost differ by type?
  5. What does the receiver observe?
  6. What beliefs does the receiver form?
  7. What action does the receiver choose?
  8. Is the proposed equilibrium separating, pooling, or semi-separating?
  9. Are incentive compatibility conditions satisfied?
  10. Are off-path beliefs reasonable?
