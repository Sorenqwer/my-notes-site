Extensive-form Games

Extensive-form games represent strategic interaction over time. They show who moves when, what each player observes, what actions are available, and what payoffs follow from each possible path.

The extensive form is especially useful for studying sequential decisions, credibility, threats, promises, commitment, entry deterrence, bargaining, signalling, and institutional processes.

Why extensive form matters

A normal-form game lists strategies and payoffs. An extensive-form game shows the structure of play.

This matters because timing can change the strategic result. If one player moves first and another responds, the second player’s credible response shapes the first player’s decision.

Basic components

An extensive-form game usually includes:

Component Meaning
Players who makes decisions
Nodes points where a decision or event occurs
Root the initial node
Branches available actions
Terminal nodes endpoints of the game
Payoffs outcomes assigned at terminal nodes
Information sets what a player can distinguish when choosing
Strategies complete plans for every possible decision point

The game tree is the visual representation of these objects.

A simple game tree

This tree says:

  1. Player 1 chooses whether to enter.
  2. If Player 1 stays out, the game ends.
  3. If Player 1 enters, Player 2 chooses whether to fight or accommodate.
  4. Payoffs are assigned at the terminal nodes.

Histories

A history is a sequence of actions that has occurred so far.

In the entry game above, after Player 1 chooses Enter, the history is:

\[ h = \text{Enter}. \]

After Player 1 chooses Stay Out, the game ends.

Histories matter because strategies in dynamic games are contingent plans. A player may choose different actions after different histories.

Strategies in extensive-form games

A strategy is not just one action. It is a complete plan.

If Player 2 moves only after entry, then Player 2’s strategy must specify what to do after entry:

\[ s_2(\text{Enter}) \in \{\text{Fight},\text{Accommodate}\}. \]

In more complex games, a strategy must specify what the player would do at every information set where they may be called to move.

This is one of the most common sources of confusion in dynamic games.

Perfect information

A game has perfect information if every player observes all previous actions before choosing.

Chess is a classic example. Each player observes the whole history of moves.

In perfect-information games, decision nodes are not grouped into non-trivial information sets. Players know exactly where they are in the tree.

Imperfect information

A game has imperfect information if a player does not know exactly which node they are at when choosing.

This is represented by an information set.

For example, if Player 2 cannot observe whether Player 1 chose \(A\) or \(B\), then Player 2’s two decision nodes are in the same information set.

The dotted connection means Player 2 cannot distinguish the two nodes.

Backward induction

In finite games with perfect information, backward induction solves the game from the end to the beginning.

Backward induction is the natural method for extensive-form games because earlier players anticipate later rational behaviour.

Credible threats

A threat is credible only if the player would actually want to carry it out when the time comes.

In the entry game, suppose Player 2 threatens to fight if Player 1 enters. If fighting gives Player 2 a lower payoff than accommodating after entry, then the threat is not credible.

This is why Nash equilibrium alone may be too weak for dynamic games.

Subgames

A subgame is a part of the game that begins at a decision node and contains all future actions from that node onward.

A subgame must not cut across an information set.

Subgames matter because a dynamic equilibrium should prescribe optimal behaviour after every possible history.

This leads to subgame perfect equilibrium.

Normal form versus extensive form

Every finite extensive-form game can be represented in normal form by listing each player’s complete strategies.

However, this conversion often hides timing.

Representation Strength Limitation
Extensive form shows timing and information can be visually large
Normal form shows all strategy profiles and payoffs hides credibility and sequence

For dynamic games, extensive form is usually better for reasoning.

Example: entry deterrence

Suppose an entrant decides whether to enter a market. If it enters, the incumbent chooses whether to fight or accommodate.

Solve by backward induction:

  1. If entry occurs, the incumbent compares Fight and Accommodate.
  2. The incumbent prefers Accommodate because \(1>-1\).
  3. The entrant anticipates Accommodate.
  4. The entrant compares Enter, giving \(2\), with Stay Out, giving \(0\).
  5. The entrant enters.

The subgame perfect outcome is:

\[ (\text{Enter}, \text{Accommodate}). \]

Dynamic games with chance

Some extensive-form games include nature. Nature represents random events.

For example, nature may choose a player’s type before the player acts.

This structure appears in signaling games and Bayesian extensive-form games.

Strategies and off-path behaviour

In an extensive-form game, a strategy must specify what to do even after histories that do not occur in equilibrium.

These histories are called off-path.

Off-path behaviour matters because it can support or undermine threats and promises.

For example, if entry does not occur in equilibrium, the incumbent’s planned response after entry is still part of the strategy.

Common mistakes

1. Solving from the root first

For finite perfect-information games, solve from the end backward.

2. Confusing action and strategy

A strategy is a complete plan, not just the action taken on the equilibrium path.

3. Ignoring off-path actions

Dynamic equilibrium requires behaviour after possible histories, even if they are not reached.

4. Treating all Nash equilibria as credible

Some Nash equilibria rely on non-credible threats.

5. Cutting across information sets

A subgame cannot begin inside an information set.

Checklist

When analysing an extensive-form game, ask:

  1. Who moves first?
  2. Who moves later?
  3. What does each player observe?
  4. What are the histories?
  5. What are the terminal payoffs?
  6. What are each player’s strategies?
  7. Are there information sets?
  8. Are there proper subgames?
  9. What does backward induction predict?
  10. Are threats and promises credible?
