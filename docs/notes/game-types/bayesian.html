Bayesian Games

Bayesian games study strategic interaction under incomplete information. Players may be uncertain about other players’ preferences, payoffs, costs, abilities, intentions, information, or types.

The key idea is that players do not simply choose actions. They choose actions while holding beliefs about what kind of opponents they are facing.

Complete versus incomplete information

A game has complete information if the structure of the game, including payoffs and types, is commonly known.

A game has incomplete information if at least one player is uncertain about another player’s type or payoff-relevant characteristic.

Incomplete information does not mean players know nothing. It means they reason with probability.

Types

A type represents private information about a player.

A type can describe:

  • cost;
  • ability;
  • valuation;
  • patience;
  • honesty;
  • bias;
  • preference;
  • information;
  • intention;
  • risk;
  • productivity.

For example, a firm may be high-cost or low-cost:

\[ \theta_i \in \{H,L\}. \]

The player’s payoff may depend on their own type and possibly on others’ types.

Type spaces

Each player has a type space.

For player \(i\), the type space is written as

\[ \Theta_i. \]

A type is an element:

\[ \theta_i \in \Theta_i. \]

A profile of types is

\[ \theta = (\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n). \]

The types of everyone except player \(i\) are written as

\[ \theta_{-i}. \]

Beliefs

Players hold beliefs over types.

If player 2 may be high-cost or low-cost, player 1 may believe:

\[ \Pr(\theta_2=H)=p, \]

and

\[ \Pr(\theta_2=L)=1-p. \]

Beliefs are central because players choose actions based on expected payoffs.

Nature

A Bayesian game is often represented as a game where nature first draws types.

This converts incomplete information into imperfect information about nature’s move.

Strategy in a Bayesian game

A strategy in a Bayesian game is a type-contingent plan.

Player \(i\)’s strategy maps types into actions:

\[ s_i:\Theta_i \to A_i. \]

This means each type of player \(i\) may choose a different action.

For example:

\[ s_i(H)=a_H, \]

and

\[ s_i(L)=a_L. \]

A strategy must specify what every possible type would do.

Expected payoff

Suppose player \(i\) has type \(\theta_i\) and chooses action \(a_i\). The player is uncertain about others’ types.

The expected payoff is:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\theta_{-i}} [ u_i(a_i,a_{-i},\theta_i,\theta_{-i}) ]. \]

With finitely many types, this can be written as:

\[ \sum_{\theta_{-i}} u_i(a_i,a_{-i},\theta_i,\theta_{-i}) \Pr(\theta_{-i}\mid \theta_i). \]

The player chooses an action that maximizes expected payoff.

Bayesian Nash equilibrium

A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where every type of every player chooses a best response given beliefs and the type-contingent strategies of others.

The important phrase is:

every type of every player.

A Bayesian equilibrium is not just one optimal action per player. It is optimal behaviour for each possible type.

Example: high-cost and low-cost firm

Suppose a firm may be high-cost or low-cost.

A rival does not observe the firm’s cost type. The rival only has a prior belief:

\[ \Pr(L)=p. \]

The low-cost firm may be more willing to set a low price. Observing a low price may make the rival think the firm is more likely to be low-cost.

This is the beginning of signaling logic.

Bayesian games therefore connect naturally to signaling games.

Harsanyi transformation

The Harsanyi transformation converts incomplete information into imperfect information.

The steps are:

  1. nature chooses types according to a commonly known prior;
  2. each player observes their own type;
  3. players choose strategies;
  4. payoffs depend on actions and types.

This allows incomplete-information games to be analysed using standard game-theoretic tools.

Common prior

A common prior means that players begin from the same probability distribution over types.

They may hold different beliefs after observing different private information, but those beliefs come from the same underlying prior.

The common prior assumption is powerful but restrictive. It rules out some forms of disagreement.

Bayesian games and mixed strategies

Bayesian games and mixed strategies both use probability, but they are different.

Concept Probability describes
Mixed strategy randomization over actions
Bayesian type uncertainty about payoff-relevant characteristics
Belief subjective probability over states or types

A player may use a pure strategy in a Bayesian game. The uncertainty comes from not knowing the type.

A player may also mix within each type.

Incomplete information versus imperfect information

These terms are related but not identical.

Term Meaning
Incomplete information uncertainty about payoffs, types, or characteristics
Imperfect information uncertainty about previous moves or current node
Bayesian game standard model of incomplete information

The Harsanyi transformation turns incomplete information into imperfect information about nature’s move.

Bayesian games and communication

Once types are private, communication becomes strategic.

An informed player may try to reveal, hide, exaggerate, or manipulate information.

This leads to:

  • cheap talk;
  • signaling;
  • screening;
  • mechanism design;
  • reputation;
  • persuasion;
  • deception.

Common mistakes

1. Treating type as action

A type is private information. An action is chosen by the player.

2. Forgetting type-contingent strategies

A strategy must say what each type would do.

3. Ignoring beliefs

Bayesian games require beliefs over types.

4. Confusing mixed strategies with incomplete information

Randomizing over actions is not the same as being uncertain about types.

5. Forgetting the common prior

Many Bayesian games assume a commonly known prior distribution over types.

6. Solving only for one type

Bayesian Nash equilibrium requires optimality for every type.

Checklist

When analysing a Bayesian game, ask:

  1. Who are the players?
  2. What are the possible types?
  3. Who observes which type?
  4. What is the prior belief over types?
  5. What actions are available?
  6. How do payoffs depend on types?
  7. What is each type’s strategy?
  8. What expected payoff does each type maximize?
  9. What are the best responses?
  10. Is the strategy profile a Bayesian Nash equilibrium?
