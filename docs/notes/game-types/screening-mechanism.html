Screening and Mechanism Design

Screening and mechanism design study how an uninformed party can structure choices, contracts, rules, or menus to induce informed parties to reveal information or choose desirable actions.

In signaling, the informed side moves to reveal information.
In screening, the uninformed side designs the environment.

Basic idea

Screening begins with private information.

The uninformed side cannot directly observe type, so it offers a menu of choices. Different types may self-select into different options.

The menu works only if each type prefers the option intended for that type.

Screening versus signaling

Feature Signaling Screening
Who has private information? sender / informed agent agent / informed side
Who moves to reveal information? informed side uninformed designer creates menu
Main object signal menu or mechanism
Key condition incentive-compatible signaling self-selection

Examples:

  • signaling: worker chooses education to reveal ability;
  • screening: employer offers contracts to separate worker types.

Types and menus

Suppose there are two types:

\[ \theta \in \{H,L\}. \]

The designer offers a menu:

\[ (x_H,t_H), (x_L,t_L). \]

Each type chooses one option.

A separating menu induces:

\[ H \text{ chooses } (x_H,t_H), \]

and

\[ L \text{ chooses } (x_L,t_L). \]

Incentive compatibility

Incentive compatibility means each type prefers the option designed for that type.

For type \(H\):

\[ U_H(x_H,t_H) \geq U_H(x_L,t_L). \]

For type \(L\):

\[ U_L(x_L,t_L) \geq U_L(x_H,t_H). \]

These are self-selection constraints.

Participation constraints

Participation constraints ensure each type prefers participating to opting out.

For type \(\theta\):

\[ U_\theta(\text{assigned option}) \geq \bar U_\theta. \]

Here \(\bar U_\theta\) is the outside option.

A contract may separate types but still fail if some type refuses to participate.

Adverse selection

Adverse selection occurs when one side has private information before exchange.

Examples:

  • insurance buyers know more about their risk than insurers;
  • workers know more about ability than employers;
  • borrowers know more about default risk than lenders;
  • sellers know more about product quality than buyers.

Screening tries to design choices that reduce the problem.

Insurance screening example

An insurer may not know whether a customer is high-risk or low-risk.

It can offer a menu:

  • high premium, full coverage;
  • low premium, partial coverage.

Different risk types may select different contracts.

The menu screens types through self-selection.

Mechanism design

Mechanism design asks a broader question:

How should rules be designed so that self-interested agents produce a desired outcome?

A mechanism specifies:

  • messages or reports agents can send;
  • how reports determine allocations;
  • how transfers or payoffs are assigned.

Mechanism design is sometimes called reverse game theory because we start with the desired outcome and design the game.

Revelation principle

The revelation principle says, roughly:

if an outcome can be implemented by some mechanism, it can often be implemented by a direct mechanism where agents truthfully report their types.

This does not mean people are naturally truthful. It means the mechanism can be designed so that truth-telling is incentive compatible.

A direct mechanism asks agents to report types:

\[ \hat\theta_i. \]

Truthful reporting requires:

\[ \theta_i \text{ reports } \theta_i \]

to be optimal.

Incentive compatibility in direct mechanisms

A direct mechanism is incentive compatible if every type prefers truthful reporting.

For type \(\theta\):

\[ U_\theta(\text{outcome after reporting } \theta) \geq U_\theta(\text{outcome after reporting } \hat\theta) \]

for all possible misreports \(\hat\theta\).

This is the same self-selection idea expressed through reports.

Applications

Screening and mechanism design appear in:

Domain Example
insurance separating high-risk and low-risk customers
labour contracts for different ability or effort types
regulation inducing firms to reveal costs
auctions allocating goods to high-valuation bidders
public goods eliciting willingness to pay
taxation designing incentives under private income or effort
criminal justice plea bargains under private evidence or risk
platforms rules that induce reporting or moderation

Screening and criminology

Screening logic can also appear in criminology and institutions.

Examples:

  • police use behaviour or context to infer risk;
  • courts use plea decisions to infer case strength;
  • offenders use tests to screen co-offender reliability;
  • platforms design reporting systems to distinguish abuse from noise;
  • regulators design audits to induce compliance.

The same logic applies: private information creates selection problems.

Common mistakes

1. Confusing screening with signaling

Screening is designed by the uninformed side. Signaling is chosen by the informed side.

2. Forgetting participation constraints

A menu must be acceptable, not only separating.

3. Ignoring incentive compatibility

A proposed menu fails if types want to imitate each other.

4. Assuming truth-telling without mechanism design

Truthful reporting must be made optimal.

5. Treating the revelation principle as behavioural honesty

The revelation principle is a design result, not a psychological claim.

Checklist

When analysing screening or mechanism design, ask:

  1. Who has private information?
  2. Who designs the menu or mechanism?
  3. What are the possible types?
  4. What options are offered?
  5. What is each type supposed to choose?
  6. Are incentive compatibility constraints satisfied?
  7. Are participation constraints satisfied?
  8. What outcome does the designer want?
  9. Can truthful reporting be made optimal?
  10. What information is revealed by choices?
