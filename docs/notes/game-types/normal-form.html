Normal-form Games

A normal-form game represents a strategic situation in which players choose strategies and receive payoffs from the resulting strategy profile. It is the standard representation for simultaneous-move games and the natural starting point for Nash equilibrium.

The normal form is useful because it makes the basic ingredients of a game explicit: players, strategies, and payoffs.

Basic structure

A normal-form game can be written as

\[ G=\langle N,(S_i)_{i\in N},(u_i)_{i\in N}\rangle. \]

Here:

Object Meaning
\(N\) set of players
\(S_i\) strategy set of player \(i\)
\(u_i\) payoff function of player \(i\)

A strategy profile is

\[ s=(s_1,\ldots,s_n). \]

Player \(i\)’s payoff is

\[ u_i(s_i,s_{-i}), \]

where \(s_{-i}\) denotes the strategies of all players other than \(i\).

Why normal form matters

Normal form gives the cleanest setting for best-response reasoning.

Once the normal-form game is defined, the main question is:

Which strategies are best responses to which other strategies?

Payoff matrix

For two-player finite games, the normal form is often shown as a payoff matrix.

For example:

Player 2: L Player 2: R
Player 1: U \((2,2)\) \((0,3)\)
Player 1: D \((3,0)\) \((1,1)\)

Each cell gives payoffs in the form:

\[ (\text{Player 1 payoff}, \text{Player 2 payoff}). \]

If Player 1 chooses \(U\) and Player 2 chooses \(L\), the outcome is \((2,2)\).

Simultaneous does not always mean literal same time

A normal-form game is often called simultaneous-move, but the key idea is informational rather than physical.

Players do not need to choose at exactly the same second. What matters is that a player cannot condition their current action on the other player’s current action.

For example:

  • two firms set prices without observing each other’s price;
  • two suspects decide whether to confess;
  • two countries choose military investment levels;
  • two students choose how much effort to contribute to a joint project.

Best responses

A best response is a strategy that maximizes a player’s payoff given the strategies of others.

For player \(i\),

\[ BR_i(s_{-i}) \in \arg\max_{s_i\in S_i} u_i(s_i,s_{-i}). \]

This says:

hold the other players’ strategies fixed, then choose the strategy that gives the highest payoff.

Best-response analysis is the core method for solving normal-form games.

Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where every player is choosing a best response to the others.

Formally, \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium if, for every player \(i\),

\[ u_i(s_i^*,s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i,s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for all } s_i\in S_i. \]

Equivalently,

\[ s_i^* \in BR_i(s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for every } i. \]

Solving a 2-by-2 game

For a two-player, two-action game, use this workflow:

A cell is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium if both players’ strategies are best responses in that cell.

Dominant strategies

A strategy is strictly dominant if it gives a player a higher payoff than every other strategy, no matter what the other players do.

For player \(i\), strategy \(s_i\) strictly dominates \(s_i'\) if

\[ u_i(s_i,s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i',s_{-i}) \]

for all \(s_{-i}\).

A weakly dominant strategy gives at least as high a payoff in every case and a strictly higher payoff in at least one case.

Dominant strategies are powerful because they do not depend on beliefs about what others do.

Dominated strategies

A strategy is dominated if another strategy always performs better.

Dominated strategies are often removed through iterative elimination.

This process is useful, but it is not the same as finding all Nash equilibria.

Coordination games

A coordination game has multiple equilibria, and players prefer to match each other’s actions.

Example:

L R
U \((2,2)\) \((0,0)\)
D \((0,0)\) \((1,1)\)

There are two Nash equilibria:

\[ (U,L) \]

and

\[ (D,R). \]

The problem is not conflict alone. The problem is selecting the same equilibrium.

Prisoners’ dilemma

The prisoners’ dilemma shows how individually rational behaviour can lead to a collectively worse outcome.

The usual structure is:

Cooperate Defect
Cooperate good for both sucker payoff
Defect temptation payoff bad for both

Each player has an incentive to defect, but mutual defection is worse than mutual cooperation.

This is why Nash equilibrium should not be confused with social optimality.

Mixed strategies

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies.

If Player 1 chooses \(U\) with probability \(p\) and \(D\) with probability \(1-p\), then Player 1’s mixed strategy is

\[ \sigma_1=(p,1-p). \]

Mixed strategies are useful when no pure-strategy equilibrium exists or when players need to keep opponents indifferent.

A mixed-strategy equilibrium requires each player to be willing to randomize among the strategies used with positive probability.

Expected payoff

If Player 2 plays \(L\) with probability \(q\) and \(R\) with probability \(1-q\), Player 1’s expected payoff from \(U\) is

\[ U_1(U,q) = q u_1(U,L)+(1-q)u_1(U,R). \]

Player 1’s expected payoff from \(D\) is

\[ U_1(D,q) = q u_1(D,L)+(1-q)u_1(D,R). \]

If Player 1 mixes between \(U\) and \(D\), then they must be indifferent:

\[ U_1(U,q)=U_1(D,q). \]

This condition is often used to solve mixed equilibria.

Normal form and extensive form

A dynamic game can often be converted into normal form by listing each player’s complete strategies.

However, the normal form may hide timing and credibility.

Representation Strength Weakness
Normal form clear strategy-payoff structure hides timing
Extensive form shows sequence and information can be larger and more complex

For sequential games, the extensive form is usually better for reasoning about credibility and subgame perfection.

Common mistakes

1. Confusing actions with strategies

In static games, an action and strategy may look similar. In dynamic games, a strategy is a complete plan.

2. Reading payoffs in the wrong order

In a payoff pair \((a,b)\), the first number usually belongs to Player 1 and the second to Player 2.

3. Treating Nash equilibrium as the best social outcome

A Nash equilibrium may be inefficient.

4. Removing strategies incorrectly

Only dominated strategies should be removed under dominance reasoning.

5. Ignoring mixed strategies

A game may have no pure equilibrium but still have a mixed equilibrium.

Checklist

When analysing a normal-form game, ask:

  1. Who are the players?
  2. What are their strategies?
  3. What are the payoffs?
  4. What is each player’s best response to each opponent strategy?
  5. Are there dominant or dominated strategies?
  6. Are there mutual best responses?
  7. Are there multiple equilibria?
  8. Is there a mixed equilibrium?
  9. Is the equilibrium efficient?
  10. What does the result mean substantively?
