Moral psychology

Moral psychology studies how people judge right and wrong, and why they enforce norms. It connects directly to: - punishment and forgiveness, - legitimacy and compliance, - polarization and “moralized” conflict.

This page uses a mechanism-first approach: moral intuitions + emotion + identity → judgments and enforcement.

Two systems (a useful simplification)

Moral judgment often reflects interaction between: - fast intuitions (emotion and gut reactions), - slower reasoning (justification, consistency checks).

Reasoning is important, but often functions as: - clarification and coherence, - persuasion, - post-hoc explanation.

Pitfall: assuming that moral disagreement is mostly about facts. Often it’s about values, identity, and perceived intent.

Key objects in moral cognition

Harm and care

People react strongly to perceived harm, especially intentional harm.

Prediction pattern: perceived intentionality increases blame and punishment.

Fairness and reciprocity

Unfairness triggers anger and punitive motivation. Reciprocity supports cooperation.

Prediction pattern: people punish unfairness even when punishment is costly.

Loyalty and group-boundaries

Group membership shapes whose suffering is salient and whose claims are credible.

Prediction pattern: moral judgments become harsher when out-groups are seen as threatening or disrespectful.

Authority and legitimacy

People often treat legitimate authority as morally relevant: - “rules exist for a reason” - “respect the process”

Prediction pattern: procedural fairness increases voluntary compliance.

Purity and contamination (where relevant)

Some moral judgments are linked to disgust and boundary maintenance.

Prediction pattern: disgust can increase harshness even when harm is unclear.

Emotion as the engine of moral enforcement

Moral emotions drive action: - anger → punishment and confrontation - disgust → exclusion and distancing - shame → concealment and conformity pressure - guilt → repair and apology - contempt → status downgrading and dehumanization risk

Prediction pattern: when anger is high, people over-attribute intentionality and demand harsher sanctions.

Intentions matter (often more than outcomes)

People judge harm differently based on: - intentional vs accidental, - negligent vs unavoidable, - provoked vs unprovoked, - foreseeable vs unforeseeable.

Prediction pattern: giving clear evidence of constraints reduces blame (shifts from “bad person” to “bad situation”).

Moralization: when preferences become sacred

Some issues become “moralized”: - disagreement feels like betrayal, - compromise feels dirty, - opponents feel dangerous.

Prediction patterns - moralized issues polarize more strongly than non-moral issues, - punishment and exclusion become more acceptable, - factual debate becomes less effective.

Intervention lever: de-moralize where possible by shifting from blame to shared goals and tradeoffs.

Norm enforcement: formal and informal

People enforce norms through: - gossip and reputation, - praise and shame, - exclusion, - punishment (private or public), - institutional enforcement (police, courts, HR, schools).

Prediction patterns - observability increases compliance and norm enforcement, - selective enforcement damages legitimacy, - unclear norms invite conflict over interpretation and power.

A reusable moral analysis template

When analyzing a moral conflict, specify:

  1. What norm is at stake? (harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity?)
  2. What intention is attributed? (hostile, negligent, accidental?)
  3. What emotions are active? (anger, disgust, shame, guilt?)
  4. What identity boundary is salient? (us vs them?)
  5. What enforcement channel exists? (informal vs institutional)
  6. Prediction: what changes if you change transparency, fairness, or face-saving options?

Canonical examples (stubs you can expand)

Example A: Punishment demand after perceived disrespect

  • small disrespect is interpreted as intentional threat,
  • anger rises,
  • punitive preference increases.

Mechanisms: threat interpretation + anger + status concern.

Example B: Legitimacy-based compliance

  • people comply because noncompliance feels wrong,
  • not just because punishment is feared.

Mechanisms: authority legitimacy + moral identity + guilt/shame.
