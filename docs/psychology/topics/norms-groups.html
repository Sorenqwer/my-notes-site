Norms & groups

Groups change psychology. People do not only act as individuals; they act as members of “us” and “them,” under shared expectations about what is normal and what is acceptable.

This page is organized as: identity + norms + social dynamics → predictions.

Core concepts

Groups and identity

A group can be defined by: - membership (formal or informal), - shared symbols (language, style, rituals), - shared fate (common outcomes), - shared narratives (“who we are”).

Identity is the psychological meaning of group membership: - it shapes attention (what matters), - it shapes belief credibility (who is trusted), - it shapes motivation (what feels worth doing), - it shapes emotion (pride, shame, anger).

Norms

Norms are shared expectations about behavior.

Two useful types: - Descriptive norms: what people actually do (“most people recycle”). - Injunctive norms: what people approve (“people should recycle”).

They can move in different directions.

Pitfall: using one as evidence for the other.

Mechanism 1: Conformity and informational reliance

People conform for at least two reasons: - Informational: “Others probably know something I don’t.” - Social: “I want approval / I want to avoid rejection.”

Prediction patterns - Conformity increases with uncertainty and ambiguity. - Conformity increases when the group is cohesive and salient. - Conformity decreases when private dissent feels safe.

Intervention levers - make independent judgment easier (private responses), - increase clarity (reduce ambiguity), - highlight diversity of behavior (break false consensus).

Mechanism 2: Social identity and in-group favoritism

Once “us vs them” is salient, people often: - interpret in-group behavior more charitably, - interpret out-group behavior more suspiciously, - allocate benefits to in-group members, - enforce harsher standards on out-groups.

Prediction patterns - Shared identity salience increases cooperation inside the group. - Strong subgroup identity can increase intergroup hostility. - Threat cues amplify “us vs them” categorization.

Intervention levers - superordinate identity (“we are all part of …”), - cross-cutting ties (friendships across groups), - institutional norms that reward fairness across boundaries.

Mechanism 3: Polarization and motivated reasoning (group-based)

Group identity can shape beliefs: - People accept in-group narratives more readily. - Evidence from out-group sources is discounted. - Mixed evidence can increase confidence rather than reduce it (biased assimilation).

Prediction patterns - Polarization rises when status or moral identity is threatened. - Argument quality matters less than source identity in highly polarized contexts. - “Correcting misinformation” can backfire if it signals out-group dominance.

Intervention levers - trusted in-group messengers, - identity-safe framing (“you can believe X and still be one of us”), - shared goals that reorient attention to outcomes.

Mechanism 4: Norm enforcement and punishment

Groups often sustain norms through: - gossip, - praise/shame, - exclusion, - punishment.

This can support prosocial behavior, but it can also produce rigidity and scapegoating.

Prediction patterns - Enforcement increases when behavior is observable. - Enforcement is stronger when violations signal disrespect or threat. - If institutions are seen as illegitimate, informal enforcement rises (sometimes violently).

Intervention levers - increase procedural fairness (legitimacy), - make pro-social behavior visible, - reduce ambiguous “gray-zone” rules that invite selective punishment.

Mechanism 5: Social dilemmas (cooperation problems)

Many group outcomes involve: - individual incentives to free-ride, - collective need for cooperation, - difficulty monitoring contributions.

Prediction patterns - Cooperation increases with repeated interaction and observability. - Cooperation increases when norms are salient and shared identity is strong. - Cooperation collapses when people believe others are defecting (expectation cascades).

Intervention levers - transparency (credible monitoring), - low-cost signals of commitment, - “conditional cooperation” design (make cooperation the default once enough others do).

A reusable explanation template

To explain a group outcome, specify:

  1. Group boundaries: who is “us” vs “them”?
  2. Norms: what is descriptive vs injunctive?
  3. Observability: who sees what?
  4. Sanctions: how are norms enforced (formal vs informal)?
  5. Threat & status: is identity/status at stake?
  6. Prediction: what shifts if identity/norm salience changes?

Canonical examples (stubs you can expand)

Example A: False consensus / pluralistic ignorance

  • Many privately disagree with a norm.
  • Everyone believes others support it.
  • Public conformity persists.

Mechanisms: social fear + misperceived descriptive norms.

Example B: Escalation between groups

  • ambiguous act → hostile interpretation,
  • retaliation → confirms hostility,
  • norms shift toward aggression.

Mechanisms: threat sensitivity + attribution + norm enforcement.

Connections
