Addiction & compulsion

This page is a mechanism map for persistent behaviors that feel hard to stop: substance use, gambling-like reinforcement patterns, compulsive checking, and other cue-driven cycles.

It is not a diagnostic guide. The goal is to understand loops: cue → craving/urge → action → reward/relief → learning → stronger cue control.

The core loop

A compact loop that explains many compulsive patterns:

  1. Cue (place, emotion, people, device, time)
  2. Urge/craving (state shift: attention narrows)
  3. Action (routine)
  4. Reward or relief (positive or negative reinforcement)
  5. Learning (habit strengthens; cues gain power)
  6. Aftermath (guilt/shame/stress may create new cues)

Many interventions work by breaking the loop at (1) or (2), not at (4).

Mechanism 1: Reinforcement (reward and relief)

Two reinforcement channels matter:

  • Positive reinforcement: behavior produces pleasure/reward.
  • Negative reinforcement: behavior removes discomfort (stress, anxiety, withdrawal, boredom).

Negative reinforcement is often the “stickiness engine” because relief is immediate.

Prediction patterns - stress increases craving and relapse risk (more need for relief). - delayed costs are discounted; immediate relief dominates. - inconsistent rewards can create persistent repetition.

Mechanism 2: Cue reactivity and attentional capture

Over time, cues become powerful: - they trigger urges automatically, - they capture attention (“tunnel vision”), - they reduce working memory available for self-control.

Prediction patterns - exposure to cues increases cravings even without conscious intent. - “willpower” fails more in cue-rich environments.

Mechanism 3: Habit dominance under stress

Under stress, fatigue, and sleep loss: - executive function decreases, - habits dominate, - people revert to default routines.

Prediction pattern: relapse often follows stress spikes or depletion states, even if motivation remains high.

Mechanism 4: Identity and shame loops

After repeated failure, people can form an identity narrative: - “this is who I am,” - “I can’t change,” - “people will judge me.”

Shame often increases concealment and isolation, which reduces support and increases stress.

Prediction patterns - stigma reduces help-seeking. - social support and identity-safe environments increase sustained change.

Mechanism 5: Social networks and norms

Behavior is shaped by: - peer availability and models, - shared routines, - norms about use and “what counts as normal.”

Prediction pattern: changing social context can matter as much as changing individual motivation.

Interventions as loop-breaking (mechanism → lever)

Break at cues (1)

  • remove triggers,
  • change routes, routines, environments,
  • add friction (extra steps),
  • block access during vulnerable times.

Break at urge (2)

  • urge-surfing (delay the action),
  • emotion regulation strategies,
  • alternative relief (exercise, connection, structured activity),
  • “if-then” implementation intentions.

Break at action-to-reward mapping (3–4)

  • reduce reward intensity/availability,
  • increase immediate costs (commitment contracts),
  • replace routine with a lower-harm substitute.

Break at aftermath (6)

  • reduce shame and isolation,
  • build repair scripts (“slip ≠ collapse”),
  • increase support and accountability.

Key principle: if an intervention relies only on motivation, it will fail in cue-rich + high-stress environments.

A reusable analysis template

When analyzing a compulsive pattern, specify:

  1. Cues: what triggers it (external + internal states)?
  2. Reinforcement: reward or relief? immediate or delayed?
  3. Habit strength: how automatic is the routine?
  4. Stress/sleep: what states amplify it?
  5. Social context: who/what environment sustains it?
  6. Loop break: which link can realistically be weakened?
