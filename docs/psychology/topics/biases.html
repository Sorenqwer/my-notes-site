Biases & heuristics

Biases are systematic patterns in judgment and choice. A useful framing:

Many biases are not “bad preferences”; they are side-effects of cognition under constraints
(limited attention, limited memory, limited computation, noisy feedback).

This page is organized as mechanism → prediction rather than a long list.

Two categories that prevent confusion

Judgment vs choice

  • Judgment biases: errors in beliefs (probabilities, frequencies, causal stories).
  • Choice biases: shifts in preferences or decisions due to framing, defaults, and context.

A pattern can be both.

Bias vs noise

  • Bias: systematic direction (predictable).
  • Noise: randomness (unpredictable variability).

Reducing noise is often easier than “removing” bias.

Mechanism 1: Availability (retrieval-driven judgment)

People judge frequency/risk by what comes to mind easily.

Prediction patterns - vivid events feel more common than they are, - recent events dominate risk perception, - repeated exposure increases perceived truth (“familiarity = credibility”).

Common pitfall: interpreting availability as “irrational” when the environment is genuinely changing (recency may be informative).

Mechanism 2: Representativeness (pattern-matching)

People assess likelihood by similarity to a stereotype or prototype.

Prediction patterns - base rates are neglected when similarity cues are strong, - small samples are treated as representative (overconfidence), - people see “streaks” in randomness.

Testable intervention: making base rates salient reduces the effect.

Mechanism 3: Anchoring (starting-point dependence)

Numerical judgments are pulled toward an initial value, even if arbitrary.

Prediction patterns - first numbers in negotiation or estimation shape outcomes, - adjustments are insufficient, especially under time pressure.

Intervention: generate your own anchor first, or force multiple independent estimates.

Mechanism 4: Framing (representation changes choice)

Equivalent descriptions can change the perceived problem.

Prediction patterns - gains vs losses change risk-taking, - “per 100 people” vs “percentage” changes intuitions, - order and grouping of options change perceived attractiveness.

Practical note: framing is often more about attention and categorization than deep preference change.

Mechanism 5: Confirmation and asymmetric skepticism

People search for evidence that supports what they already think, and scrutinize opposing evidence more harshly.

Prediction patterns - polarization increases with identity-relevant topics, - mixed evidence leads to stronger prior beliefs (biased assimilation), - people become more confident after consuming selective information.

Intervention: force consideration of strong counterarguments; pre-commit to what evidence would change your mind.

Mechanism 6: Overconfidence and miscalibration

People often overestimate accuracy and underestimate uncertainty.

Prediction patterns - too-narrow confidence intervals, - “illusion of control” in noisy environments, - planning fallacy (systematic underestimation of time/cost).

Intervention: reference class forecasting (use similar past projects as priors).

Mechanism 7: Status quo, defaults, and friction

Doing nothing is often a decision rule.

Prediction patterns - defaults dominate choices when attention is scarce, - small “hassle costs” change behavior a lot, - opt-in vs opt-out designs shift participation dramatically.

Interpretation: not necessarily preference; often an attention/effort allocation outcome.

Mechanism 8: Affect and arousal (emotion as a driver)

Feelings shape what seems risky, urgent, or morally acceptable.

Prediction patterns - fear increases threat sensitivity and avoidance, - anger increases punitive choices and perceived intentionality, - stress increases habit reliance and narrows attention.

Links: this connects directly to Emotion & motivation.

A compact “bias debugging” checklist

When you see a bias-like pattern, ask: 1) Attention: what information is salient vs hidden? 2) Memory: what examples come to mind? 3) Computation: is the task too complex for working memory? 4) Feedback: is learning noisy, delayed, or selective? 5) Identity/emotion: is the topic threatening or status-relevant? 6) Choice architecture: are defaults/friction doing the work?

If changing (1), (3), or (5) changes behavior, it’s likely not a stable preference.
