Decision-making

Decision-making is about how people map uncertainty + preferences + constraints into choices. Psychology differs from “textbook rational choice” mainly by emphasizing: - limited attention, - limited computation, - context effects, - emotion and motivation, - social and moral preferences.

A minimal decomposition

A decision process can be separated into: 1) Representation: what options do I even see? 2) Evaluation: how do I assign value (including risk and time)? 3) Choice rule: how do I select (maximization vs satisficing vs habits)? 4) Learning: how do I update after feedback?

A lot of disagreement across theories is really disagreement about (1) and (3).

Risk: why people don’t behave like expected utility

Common psychological regularities (high-level): - Reference dependence: outcomes feel like gains/losses relative to a reference point. - Loss sensitivity: losses tend to loom larger than equally-sized gains. - Probability weighting: small probabilities may be overweighted; moderate/large may be underweighted. - Framing effects: equivalent descriptions can change choice by changing representation.

Pitfall: calling this “irrational” too early. Often it’s a mismatch between the analyst’s representation and the subject’s.

Time: present bias and self-control

When decisions involve time (study now vs game now; save vs spend): - People often behave as if they put extra weight on the present. - This creates “planner vs doer” conflicts: preferences differ across time.

Prediction pattern: commitment devices can increase follow-through even without changing incentives.

Heuristics: not just shortcuts, but strategies

Heuristics are often: - fast, - robust under noise, - adapted to common environments.

Useful lens: heuristics trade off accuracy vs cost (attention, time, computation).

Canonical example template (reuse later)

When you add examples, write them in this form: - Task: what is being decided? - Normative benchmark: what would a standard rational model predict? - Observed behavior: what pattern appears? - Mechanism: what cognitive/emotional/social process explains it? - Intervention: what changes the behavior (information, framing, incentives, commitment)?

Where this connects to the rest of the site

  • Social cognition: decisions depend on perceived intentions, norms, identity, and reputation.
  • Learning: feedback changes preferences, habits, and beliefs over time.
  • Criminology: risk/time preferences and self-control show up in offending, compliance, and deterrence responses.
  • Overlap with strategic interaction: depth limits, miscalibration about others, and social emotions change outcomes in interactive settings.
