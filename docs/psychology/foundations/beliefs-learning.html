Beliefs, uncertainty, and confidence

Beliefs are internal models of the world: what is true, how likely it is, and what causes what.
This page focuses on how beliefs form and change under limited information, limited attention, and social influence.

A key theme: > People update beliefs using evidence and psychological constraints (salience, identity, emotion, trust).

Three objects to track

When discussing beliefs, separate:

  1. Level: what do I think is true?
  2. Uncertainty: how sure am I?
  3. Calibration: does confidence match accuracy?

Many disputes are really disputes about (2) and (3).

Sources of belief

Beliefs come from: - personal experience, - testimony and communication, - institutions (official reports, rules, signals), - social networks and norms, - memory (which is reconstructive).

Prediction pattern: beliefs become “sticky” when they are tied to identity or repeated through social reinforcement.

Updating under uncertainty: why it deviates from ideal Bayesian updating

Even when people try to be rational, updating is constrained by:

1) Salience and availability

Evidence that is vivid, recent, or emotionally charged is overweighted.

Prediction pattern: salient events produce spikes in perceived risk that decay over time.

2) Selective attention and selective exposure

People seek information that fits goals and identity, and avoid costly dissonance.

Prediction pattern: information diets become polarized even without explicit propaganda.

3) Asymmetric skepticism

People scrutinize disconfirming evidence more harshly than confirming evidence.

Prediction pattern: mixed evidence can increase confidence rather than reduce it (biased assimilation).

4) Trust and source credibility

People treat sources as part of the data: - “Is this person competent?” - “Are they honest?” - “Are they on my side?”

Prediction pattern: same facts produce different belief change across trust groups.

Causal beliefs: stories beat statistics

People strongly prefer causal narratives: - “X caused Y because …” - “This type of person does …” - “This policy leads to …”

Causal learning is hard when: - multiple causes move together, - feedback is delayed, - systems adapt strategically.

Prediction pattern: people overinfer causality from small samples and salient coincidences.

Confidence: the underappreciated variable

Confidence drives behavior: - willingness to act, - willingness to punish, - willingness to speak, - openness to learning.

But confidence is often miscalibrated.

Overconfidence and underconfidence

  • Overconfidence can produce excessive risk-taking or premature commitment.
  • Underconfidence can produce paralysis and overreliance on authority.

Prediction pattern: reducing uncertainty (clearer feedback, better measurement) changes behavior even if belief levels don’t move much.

Metacognition: knowing what you know

Metacognition is the ability to monitor: - how accurate you are, - when you’re guessing, - when you need more information.

Metacognition degrades under: - stress, - time pressure, - strong emotion, - social threat.

Prediction pattern: hostile environments reduce honest uncertainty expression (“everyone must sound certain”).

Social belief dynamics: cascades and pluralistic ignorance

Beliefs spread socially, sometimes creating: - information cascades: people copy others because they assume others know more. - pluralistic ignorance: people privately disagree but assume others agree, so they stay silent.

Prediction patterns - Public signals can trigger rapid belief shifts. - Small visible minorities can shape perceived norms disproportionately.

A reusable belief analysis template

When explaining belief formation or change, specify:

  1. Evidence: what information exists and who sees it?
  2. Trust: which sources are credible to whom?
  3. Salience: what is vivid vs statistical?
  4. Identity: what beliefs are socially rewarded?
  5. Uncertainty: how confident are people, and is it calibrated?
  6. Feedback: is correction fast, slow, or absent?
  7. Prediction: what changes if you change visibility, messenger, or feedback?

Connections

  • Learning: beliefs update differently from habits; both matter.
  • Decision-making: confidence and uncertainty shape risk and action.
  • Communication overlap: trust, framing, and identity determine belief change.
  • Criminology overlap: perceived risk, legitimacy, and rumor dynamics drive cooperation and compliance.
