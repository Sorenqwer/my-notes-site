Methods

Methods are the discipline’s error-correction system: how we decide whether a psychological claim is true, how big the effect is, and when it generalizes.

This page is intentionally practical: it’s a checklist for evaluating evidence while you read papers or build arguments.

The basic problem

Psychology studies internal states (beliefs, emotions, motives) using external signals (choices, reports, physiology, behavior).
That creates two recurring challenges:

  1. Measurement: are we measuring what we think we’re measuring?
  2. Inference: does the evidence support causality or only association?

Study types (and what each can and can’t do)

1) Experiments (randomized)

Strength: best for causal inference (if implemented well).
Weakness: may be artificial; limited generalization; demand effects.

Questions to ask: - Was assignment truly random? - Was there attrition (dropout) correlated with treatment? - Was the manipulation strong and specific (not confounded)? - Are outcomes pre-specified or cherry-picked?

2) Observational studies

Strength: real-world behavior, larger samples, external validity.
Weakness: confounding, selection, reverse causality.

Questions to ask: - What could cause both X and Y? - Could Y influence X? - Are there omitted variables that change interpretation?

3) Field experiments / natural experiments

Strength: causal inference in real settings.
Weakness: messy implementation; compliance issues; spillovers.

Questions to ask: - Was treatment actually delivered (compliance)? - Were there spillovers or displacement effects? - Was the identification assumption plausible?

4) Qualitative / case-based evidence

Strength: mechanisms, context, meaning, hypothesis generation.
Weakness: hard to estimate size; risks confirmation bias.

Questions to ask: - Is the case chosen systematically or selectively? - Are rival explanations considered?

Measurement: validity beats vibes

Construct validity

Are we measuring the intended construct (e.g., “anxiety”, “trust”, “self-control”)?

  • Many constructs have multiple operationalizations.
  • Single-item measures are fragile.
  • Self-reports capture self-perception and presentation, not pure internal state.

Red flags: vague constructs + weak operational definition + strong conclusions.

Reliability

Do we get similar results if we measure again (or with equivalent items)?

  • Low reliability attenuates effects and creates noisy findings.
  • If measurement is unstable, inference becomes unstable.

A practical reading rule: > If reliability is not discussed for a key measure, treat estimates as fragile.

Demand characteristics and experimenter effects

People sometimes behave the way they think the experiment “wants.”

Mitigation ideas: - deception (when ethical), - cover stories, - unobtrusive behavioral measures, - double-blind procedures.

Causal inference: the minimum you need

Confounding (omitted variables)

If a third factor drives both X and Y, the association can be misleading.

Example schema: - trait T → both X and Y
So X and Y correlate even if X does not cause Y.

Selection

People choose into conditions (schools, jobs, groups). That choice is informative.

Example schema: - motivation → program participation + later success
So participation appears to “work” even if it’s selection.

Reverse causality

Y can cause X (or they co-evolve).

Mediation vs moderation (common confusion)

  • Mediator: X changes M, which changes Y (mechanism pathway).
  • Moderator: the effect of X on Y differs by Z (heterogeneity).

Quick test:
Mediator is “how”; moderator is “when/for whom.”

Effect sizes: “statistically significant” is not “important”

Always separate: - direction (positive/negative), - magnitude (how big), - precision (confidence interval), - practical relevance (does it matter in the real world?).

Reading habit: if a paper only reports p-values without effect sizes and uncertainty, downgrade confidence.

Replicability and generalization (the two big filters)

Replication

Can the effect be reproduced in a new sample with the same procedure?

Treat “one study” as: - suggestive evidence, not a conclusion.

Generalization

Does it hold across: - populations (age, culture), - settings (lab vs field), - measurement (different instruments), - incentives/stakes?

A useful mental model: > Effects can be real but local.

Common failure modes (how to spot weak evidence)

  • Many outcomes tested, only the “wins” reported (multiple comparisons).
  • Researcher degrees of freedom (flexible stopping rules, model choices).
  • Post-hoc storytelling (hypotheses invented after results are known).
  • Small samples with big claims.
  • Convenience samples presented as universal human psychology.

This does not mean “ignore everything,” but it means “calibrate confidence.”

A practical checklist for any claim

When you read a claim, ask:

  1. What exactly is the construct?
  2. How is it measured? reliable? valid?
  3. What is the identification strategy? randomization? controls? instruments?
  4. What are alternative explanations? confounding? selection? reverse causality?
  5. How big is the effect? and is it meaningful?
  6. Does it replicate?
  7. Where does it generalize?

If a paper passes (1)–(4), it’s already unusually strong.

Connections to other sections

Decision-making, learning, social cognition

Methods tell you which behavioral patterns are robust and which are artifacts of measurement or sampling.

Criminology

Measurement and selection issues are central (reporting bias, enforcement bias, displacement/spillovers).
A methods mindset prevents “policy conclusions” from being driven by selection.

Overlaps (later)

Many strategic-behavior claims (trust, deterrence, cooperation) hinge on whether you can distinguish: - beliefs vs preferences, - selection vs treatment, - correlation vs causality.
