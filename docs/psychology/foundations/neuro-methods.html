Neuroscience methods

This page explains what common neuroscience methods can and cannot tell you, in a way that stays useful even if specific technologies change.

A simple rule: > Methods answer different questions: where, when, what correlates, what causes, what is necessary.

Neuro evidence is powerful when it constrains theories — not when it replaces them with “brain pictures.”

1) What neuro methods are for (the question map)

Neuroscience methods are often used to answer:

  • Localization: where in the brain does activity relate to a task?
  • Timing: when do processes occur (early perception vs later evaluation)?
  • Necessity: is a region/system required for a function?
  • Causality: what happens when we perturb a system?
  • Mechanism constraints: does the brain implement your theory in a plausible way?

A good paper makes clear which of these it’s claiming.

2) fMRI (functional magnetic resonance imaging)

What it measures (conceptually)

fMRI measures changes related to blood oxygenation (a proxy for neural activity), with a delay.

What it is good for

  • spatial patterns (rough “where”)
  • comparing conditions (task A vs task B)
  • linking networks to tasks

What it is not good for

  • fine-grained timing (“when exactly”)
  • proving causality by itself
  • reading thoughts (in general use)

Pitfalls - reverse inference: “region X lit up, so emotion Y happened” - ignoring that many processes share the same regions

Best use: constrain competing psychological theories by showing which network patterns align with which processes.

3) EEG / ERP (electroencephalography / event-related potentials)

What it measures

Electrical activity at the scalp, reflecting synchronous neural activity.

What it is good for

  • timing (“when” processes occur)
  • differentiating early sensory processing vs later decision stages
  • studying attention and error monitoring

Limits

  • localization is coarse (harder to say exactly “where”)
  • sensitive to noise (movement, environment)

Best use: connect cognitive stages (perception → attention → evaluation) to time.

4) MEG (magnetoencephalography) (conceptual note)

MEG measures magnetic fields from neural activity. It is often treated as: - timing like EEG, - localization better than EEG (still not perfect).

You don’t need to emphasize it unless you later want a “methods completeness” section.

5) Lesion and patient studies

What they are

Study behavior after brain injury or neurological conditions.

What they are good for

  • necessity claims: “when this system is damaged, function F is impaired”
  • dissociations: one ability can break while another remains intact

Limits

  • lesions are rarely clean or identical across people
  • compensation and reorganization can occur
  • interpretation needs careful cognitive modeling

Best use: demonstrate constraints and dissociations that any theory must respect.

6) TMS / tDCS (brain stimulation and modulation)

TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation)

Uses magnetic pulses to temporarily disrupt or alter neural activity.

Good for - causal tests: what changes when region/system is perturbed? - mapping functional roles under controlled tasks

Limits - limited depth (mostly cortex) - effects can be variable across people - “where” and “how” claims must be careful

tDCS (transcranial direct current stimulation)

Applies weak currents; effects are often smaller and more variable.

Best use: treat as “modulatory evidence,” not decisive proof.

7) Psychophysiology (body measures)

Includes: - heart rate variability, skin conductance, pupil dilation - hormone sampling (e.g., cortisol) - breathing patterns, startle reflex

Good for - arousal and stress responses - threat sensitivity and regulation dynamics

Limits - arousal ≠ one specific emotion - social context changes physiology strongly

Best use: connect stress/arousal to attention narrowing, habit dominance, and emotional reactivity.

8) Pharmacology (conceptual)

Studying behavior under substances that modulate neurotransmitter systems.

Good for - suggesting causal involvement of neurotransmitter systems - understanding mechanism plausibility

Limits - drugs have broad effects (not clean single-lever interventions) - individual differences and context matter

Best use: triangulate with behavioral and neural evidence.

9) Animal and comparative methods (conceptual note)

Animal models allow: - invasive measurement, - precise control, - causal circuit interventions.

But translation to human cognition: - is strongest for basic learning, reward, threat, and physiology - is weaker for language, complex social institutions, and moral reasoning.

Best use: use animal evidence to support foundational mechanisms, not to “explain society.”

10) What makes neuro evidence persuasive? (triangulation)

Neuroscience is most convincing when: - behavioral findings replicate, - neural evidence matches the predicted mechanism, - perturbation/lesion evidence supports necessity/causality, - results generalize across tasks and samples.

Rule: one fMRI result is rarely decisive; converging evidence is.

Common interpretive errors (quick list)

  • Reverse inference: region activation → specific mental state (too strong)
  • Neuro-essentialism: “because brain, it’s more real”
  • Overlocalization: treating networks as isolated modules
  • Ignoring task impurity: tasks rarely measure only one mechanism
  • Causality leap: correlational neural evidence treated as causal

A reusable method-selection template

When you want to study a psychological mechanism, write:

  1. Mechanism hypothesis (algorithmic level)
  2. Behavioral predictions
  3. What would count as evidence against it
  4. Choose method by question:
    • where? fMRI
    • when? EEG/ERP
    • necessary? lesions
    • causal perturbation? TMS
    • arousal/stress? physiology/hormones
  5. Triangulate across at least two methods
