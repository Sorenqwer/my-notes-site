Learning

Learning explains how behavior changes with experience. It is not just “updating beliefs” in a rational sense; it includes associations, habits, reinforcement, imitation, and memory distortions.

A helpful split:

  • Learning what is true (belief learning, causal learning)
  • Learning what works (reinforcement learning, habit)
  • Learning what is acceptable (social learning, norms)

A minimal decomposition

Most learning processes can be described as:

  1. Signal: what feedback arrives (reward, punishment, approval, outcome)?
  2. Credit assignment: what action/cue is blamed or credited?
  3. Update rule: how strongly do we change behavior/belief?
  4. Generalization: do we apply the lesson to similar situations?
  5. Persistence: do we forget, decay, or form a habit?

Many “irrational” outcomes are really failures at (2) or distortions in (4).

Reinforcement learning: behavior shaped by reward and punishment

In reinforcement learning, choices are strengthened by outcomes: - actions followed by reward become more likely, - actions followed by punishment become less likely.

Key feature: reinforcement can shape behavior even when people cannot fully articulate why.

Prediction patterns

  • Variable rewards (unpredictable rewards) can create persistent behavior (strong habit formation).
  • Immediate feedback produces faster learning than delayed feedback.
  • Sparse feedback increases superstition-like associations (“it worked once, so it must work”).

Common pitfall

Assuming punishment always reduces behavior. In many social settings punishment can also: - increase attention and status, - increase resentment and retaliation, - teach avoidance rather than compliance.

Habit: when actions become automatic

Habits are learned action patterns triggered by cues, not by deliberation.

A useful formula: cue → routine → reward

Habits are often: - fast, - effort-saving, - stable under stress, - insensitive to new information.

Prediction pattern: people can “know better” but still repeat the behavior because the cue triggers the routine.

Changing habits

Interventions that work: - remove or change cues, - replace the routine (substitute behavior), - keep some reward (so substitution is sustainable), - reshape the environment (friction, defaults).

Belief learning: updating what you think is true

Belief learning includes: - learning base rates and frequencies, - causal learning (what causes what), - social belief learning (what others will do / what norms are).

Belief updating is often asymmetric: - people accept confirming evidence too easily, - scrutinize disconfirming evidence too harshly, - overweight vivid experiences relative to statistics.

Prediction pattern: vivid personal experiences can dominate statistical evidence, even when objectively less informative.

Social learning: imitation, norms, and “what people like us do”

A large share of human learning comes from others: - imitation, - instruction, - conformity, - prestige-biased copying (“copy the successful/high-status person”).

Two important norm channels: - descriptive norms: what most people do - injunctive norms: what people approve/disapprove

Prediction pattern: showing that “most people comply” can increase compliance more than moral appeals, but can backfire if noncompliance is framed as common.

Learning under uncertainty: exploration vs exploitation

Learners trade off: - exploitation (do what already seems best), - exploration (try alternatives to learn more).

People often explore too little when: - feedback is noisy, - losses feel especially salient, - evaluation and reputation pressure are high.

Prediction pattern: when failure is publicly punished, exploration drops and systems stagnate.

Credit assignment: the hidden driver of bad learning

Credit assignment asks: what exactly caused the outcome?

Failures occur when: - multiple factors change at once, - outcomes are delayed, - the environment is strategic (others respond), - feedback is selective (you only see some outcomes).

Prediction pattern: in strategic environments, naive learners can misattribute outcomes to their own action instead of to opponents’ adaptation.

A reusable explanation template

To explain a learned behavior, write:

  1. Environment: what feedback structure exists (immediate/delayed, noisy, sparse)?
  2. Cues: what triggers action?
  3. Update mechanism: reinforcement, habit, belief updating, social learning?
  4. Bias point: where does it go wrong (credit assignment, generalization, forgetting)?
  5. Intervention: what changes learning (feedback timing, salience, norms, friction)?

Canonical examples (stubs you can expand)

Example A: “I keep doing it even though I know it’s bad”

  • behavior is cued automatically,
  • reward is immediate (relief, pleasure),
  • costs are delayed.

Mechanisms: habit + reinforcement + present focus.

Example B: Learning in social conflict

  • one side escalates, other side responds,
  • each interprets response as confirmation of hostility,
  • both update toward distrust.

Mechanisms: biased attribution + reinforcement of defensive routines + selective memory.
