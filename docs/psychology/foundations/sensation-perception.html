Sensation & perception

Sensation is the detection of physical energy (light, sound, pressure).
Perception is the brain’s inference about what is out there.

A useful stance: > Perception is not recording. It is construction under uncertainty.

This page gives the core ideas commonly taught in UG psychology: bottom-up vs top-down, attention, and why perception can be systematically wrong.

1) The perception pipeline (minimal)

  1. Input (stimulus)
  2. Transduction (sense organs convert energy into neural signals)
  3. Early processing (basic features)
  4. Integration (objects, scenes, meaning)
  5. Interpretation (inference using context and expectations)
  6. Action (what you do with it)

Many “errors” are predictable consequences of step (5).

2) Bottom-up vs top-down processing

Bottom-up (data-driven)

Perception is driven by the stimulus: - contrast, edges, loudness, motion, timing.

Top-down (model-driven)

Perception is shaped by: - expectations, - goals, - context, - prior knowledge, - emotion and threat.

Prediction patterns - ambiguity increases top-down influence (people “see” what they expect). - stress and threat bias interpretation toward danger. - labels and categories change what is perceived (not only what is reported).

3) Signal detection: sensitivity vs decision criterion (conceptual)

In many tasks, perception involves distinguishing signal from noise.

Two separable things: - Sensitivity: how well you can detect differences - Criterion: how willing you are to say “I detect it”

Prediction patterns - when costs of misses are high, people adopt a “liberal” criterion (report more detections, more false alarms) - when costs of false alarms are high, people adopt a “conservative” criterion (fewer reports, more misses)

This is a key bridge to: - threat detection, - eyewitness identification, - security screening, - clinical symptom reporting.

4) Attention and perception: what you don’t attend to, you don’t perceive

Attention selects information for deeper processing.

Prediction patterns - inattentional blindness: people miss salient events when attention is elsewhere - change blindness: large changes can go unnoticed without attention - divided attention reduces integration and increases reliance on heuristics

Design implication: if something is critical, make it salient at the moment it matters.

5) Perceptual constancies (why perception seems stable)

The world changes (lighting, distance), but perception often remains stable: - size constancy, - color constancy, - loudness constancy.

These are achievements of inference systems that correct for context.

Prediction pattern: constancies can produce systematic illusions in unusual contexts.

6) Illusions as diagnostics

Illusions matter because they show: - what assumptions the perceptual system is using, - when top-down inference dominates.

Takeaway: perception is optimized for typical environments, not for perfect accuracy in all cases.

7) Multisensory integration (how senses combine)

The brain integrates signals across modalities: - vision + hearing for speech perception, - touch + vision for object inference.

Integration depends on: - timing alignment, - spatial alignment, - prior expectation that the sources belong together.

Prediction pattern: when signals conflict, perception can be dominated by the modality treated as more reliable in context.

8) Perception, emotion, and threat

Perception is biased by affective state: - threat increases vigilance and pessimistic interpretation of ambiguity - anxiety increases attention to threat cues - anger increases perceived intentionality and hostility

Prediction pattern: safety and predictability widen attention and improve discrimination.

This is the bridge to: - stress & hot states in criminology, - institutional encounters and legitimacy, - interpersonal conflict escalation.

A reusable perception explanation template

When analyzing a perceptual phenomenon, specify:

  1. Stimulus ambiguity: how noisy or unclear is the input?
  2. Goal state: what does the person want/avoid?
  3. Context: what expectations are activated?
  4. Attention: what is attended vs ignored?
  5. Criterion: is the person set to detect more (false alarms) or less (misses)?
  6. Prediction: what changes if you change costs, salience, or safety?

Canonical examples (stubs you can expand)

Example A: Threat detection in ambiguous environments

  • ambiguity is high,
  • costs of missing threat feel huge,
  • criterion shifts toward “better safe than sorry.”

Mechanisms: criterion shift + top-down threat bias

Example B: Eyewitness perception under stress

  • high arousal narrows attention,
  • encoding is selective,
  • later recall is reconstructive.

Mechanisms: attention narrowing + memory reconstruction
