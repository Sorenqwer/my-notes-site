Social cognition

Social cognition studies how people perceive, interpret, and predict other people—their beliefs, intentions, emotions, and future actions. It is the psychological machinery behind coordination, conflict, trust, stigma, reputation, and norms.

A useful way to think about it:
social cognition = inference about minds + inference about situations + inference about groups.

Core questions

  • What is this person like? (traits, character, identity)
  • What does this person want? (goals, incentives, motives)
  • What does this person know or believe? (information, ignorance, uncertainty)
  • What will they do next? (prediction, forecasting, response)
  • What do “people like us” do here? (norms, roles, scripts)

Mechanisms map

1) Theory of mind (mentalizing)

Theory of mind is the capacity to represent others’ beliefs and intentions, including the possibility that others hold false beliefs.

  • Low mentalizing: “They see what I see.”
  • Higher mentalizing: “They think I think …” (recursive reasoning, but limited depth in practice).

Prediction pattern: when tasks require more steps of “I think you think,” performance drops sharply, especially under time pressure or stress.

Common pitfall: treating theory of mind as all-or-nothing. In reality it varies by context, load, familiarity, and incentives.

2) Attribution: person vs situation

When explaining behavior, people often choose between: - dispositional explanations (“that’s who they are”), and - situational explanations (“that’s what the environment made likely”).

A classic regularity: people often overweight dispositions relative to situations, especially when judging others.

Prediction pattern: providing salient situational information (constraints, incentives, background) shifts judgments and reduces moralized blame.

3) Social perception: rapid, low-data inference

Humans make fast judgments from sparse cues (face, tone, posture, labels). This can be adaptive but error-prone.

Two recurring dimensions in impressions: - Warmth (intentions: friend or threat?) - Competence (ability: can they act on intentions?)

These map naturally into many social outcomes: trust, fear, respect, pity, contempt.

4) Identity and self-categorization

People do not only think as individuals; they also think as members of groups: - “people like me” - “people like them” - “what we do”

Identity changes what is salient: - what evidence feels credible, - what behavior feels appropriate, - what costs feel worth paying.

Prediction pattern: making a shared identity salient increases cooperation; making subgroup divisions salient increases conflict and misinterpretation.

5) Norms and social scripts

A huge amount of behavior is “rule-following” without explicit deliberation. Norms specify: - what is typical (descriptive norms), - what is approved (injunctive norms).

Scripts specify “what happens next” in familiar settings (classrooms, clinics, police stops, interviews).

Prediction pattern: changing perceived descriptive norms can change behavior even when incentives do not (people follow what they think most others do).

6) Reputation and indirect reciprocity

People care about how they are seen. Reputation can substitute for direct enforcement.

Key ingredients: - observability (who sees what), - interpretability (what the action means), - memory (how long it sticks), - audience incentives (who benefits from punishing or praising).

Prediction pattern: making actions public increases pro-social behavior, but can also create performative or status-seeking distortions.

Limits and distortions (important)

Social cognition is powerful but systematically constrained.

Cognitive load and stress

Under load, people: - mentalize less deeply, - rely more on stereotypes and scripts, - become more sensitive to threat cues.

Motivated reasoning

When identity, emotion, or self-image is at stake, beliefs can shift toward what is psychologically comfortable: - selective attention to confirming evidence, - asymmetric skepticism (harsh on “their” claims, lenient on “ours”), - post-hoc rationalization.

Miscalibration about others

Common misbeliefs include: - false consensus (“most people think like me”), - pluralistic ignorance (“others privately disagree but go along”), - spotlight effects (overestimating how much others notice us), - fundamental attribution tendency (over-blaming traits for others).

A reusable explanation template

When you explain a social outcome, try:

  1. Situation: what is the environment (stakes, time, observability, roles)?
  2. Inference: what does each side think the other believes/wants?
  3. Identity & norms: what categories and norms are active?
  4. Emotion: what affective state is present (fear, anger, shame)?
  5. Action: what behavior follows (cooperate, punish, withdraw, attack)?
  6. Feedback: what is learned; what reputation update occurs?

Canonical examples (stubs you can expand)

Example A: Misinterpretation cascade

  • A ambiguous action is interpreted as hostile.
  • That interpretation triggers defensive behavior.
  • The defensive behavior is interpreted as confirmation.
  • The relationship locks into conflict.

Mechanisms: attribution bias + threat sensitivity + reputation concerns.

Example B: Norm enforcement without police

  • People punish or shame norm violators even at personal cost.
  • Observers cooperate more when punishment is visible.

Mechanisms: injunctive norms + reputation + indirect reciprocity.

Connections to other sections on the site

Criminology

  • Legitimacy and compliance: trust in authorities depends on perceived intentions (warmth/fairness) and competence.
  • Reporting and cooperation: social identity, fear of retaliation, and perceived norms shape willingness to report.
  • Violence and escalation: misattribution plus threat sensitivity can produce rapid escalation.

Game-theory overlap (kept light here)

Social cognition supplies the psychological primitives behind strategic interaction: - depth-limited belief reasoning, - reputation and signaling, - norm-based preferences (approval, shame, guilt), - audience effects and public observability.
