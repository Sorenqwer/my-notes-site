Measurement

Measurement is where many psychological arguments succeed or fail.

If you want to claim “X affects Y,” you must know: - what X and Y mean operationally, - whether they are measured reliably, - whether your measure captures the construct rather than noise.

This page is deeper than Methods: it’s about how variables are built.

Constructs vs measures

A construct is the idea (e.g., “trust”, “self-control”, “anxiety”).
A measure is the operational tool (survey items, tasks, behavior logs).

One construct can have many measures, and one measure can mix constructs.

Rule: never argue from labels. Argue from operational definitions.

Validity: are you measuring what you think?

Content validity

Does the measure cover the construct’s important aspects?

Convergent / discriminant validity

  • Convergent: does it correlate with other measures of the same construct?
  • Discriminant: does it avoid correlating with unrelated constructs?

Predictive validity

Does it predict outcomes the construct should predict?

Red flag: a measure is used because it is popular, not because it is justified for the specific argument.

Reliability: is the measure stable enough to be useful?

Internal consistency (for multi-item scales)

Are items measuring the same underlying thing?

Test–retest reliability

Does the measure remain similar over time (when it should)?

Inter-rater reliability

When humans rate behavior, do they agree?

Practical implication: low reliability makes effects look unstable and encourages overfitting.

Self-report: useful but not “direct access”

Self-report captures: - introspection, - self-image, - social desirability, - memory reconstruction.

It can be excellent for private experiences, but it also has predictable distortions.

Prediction patterns - sensitive topics are underreported in low-trust settings. - social desirability rises when anonymity is low. - current mood biases reports (“state contamination”).

Design levers - anonymity and privacy, - neutral wording, - indirect questioning when appropriate, - multiple methods (triangulation).

Behavioral tasks: what you gain and what you risk

Tasks can reduce self-presentation bias but may be sensitive to: - instructions and framing, - motivation and effort, - learning during the task, - “task impurity” (multiple mechanisms affect performance).

Rule of thumb: interpret tasks as measures of performance under specific conditions, not pure traits.

Physiological measures: strong signals, hard interpretation

Physiology (heart rate, skin conductance, etc.) can indicate arousal but rarely maps 1:1 to a single emotion.

Pitfall: “arousal = fear” (often false).

Measurement invariance (cross-group comparability)

If you compare groups (cultures, genders, age), ask: - do items mean the same thing? - do response styles differ (acquiescence, extremity, modesty)?

Prediction pattern: apparent group differences can be measurement artifacts.

Common measurement errors (and fixes)

Range restriction

If everyone scores near the top or bottom, correlations shrink.

Floor/ceiling effects

The measure can’t detect change.

Reactivity

Measuring behavior changes behavior (people perform for the measure).

Demand characteristics

People infer the hypothesis and comply or resist.

Fix set: pretest, blind conditions, unobtrusive outcomes, manipulate checks, and triangulate.

A practical checklist

Before using a variable, write:

  1. Construct definition (one sentence)
  2. Operationalization (exact items/task/behavior)
  3. Reliability evidence (or why it’s acceptable without it)
  4. Validity argument (why this measure matches the construct)
  5. Likely biases (social desirability, reactivity, invariance)
  6. A robustness plan (alternate measures or sensitivity tests)

Connections

  • Methods: measurement quality determines inference quality.
  • Mental health / stigma: self-report is especially fragile under shame/fear.
  • Criminology: reporting and enforcement are measurement systems with selection bias.
  • Institutions: procedural justice shapes willingness to report truthfully.
