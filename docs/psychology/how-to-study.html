How to study psychology

Psychology is a discipline about mechanisms: how minds and social systems generate behavior. To study it well, you want to avoid two traps:

  • Trap A (storytelling): “That sounds plausible” without testable predictions.
  • Trap B (list memorization): collecting terms without understanding mechanisms.

This guide gives a workflow for reading, note-making, and building explanations that connect to the rest of your site.

1) The core objects psychology tries to explain

Most psychological explanations target one (or more) of:

  • Behavior: actions, choices, habits, compliance, aggression, helping
  • Beliefs: what people think is true; confidence; uncertainty; trust in sources
  • Emotion: fear, anger, shame, guilt, motivation, arousal
  • Cognition: attention, memory, mental models, reasoning limits
  • Social interaction: trust, norms, identity, reputation, conflict and repair

When reading, ask: which object is this paper actually explaining?

2) Mechanisms first: a reusable template

Write every explanation in the same skeleton:

  1. Input: what information / stimulus / context is present?
  2. Representation: what does the person think the situation is?
  3. Process: what mechanism runs? (attention, habit, appraisal, attribution, etc.)
  4. Constraint: what limits apply? (working memory, stress, incentives, identity threat)
  5. Output: what behavior/belief/emotion results?
  6. Prediction: what changes if you vary the input or constraints?

If a claim has no (6), it’s not yet a good explanation.

3) Evidence hierarchy (practical, not dogmatic)

Different questions need different evidence:

  • Experiments: strongest for causality (if design is clean)
  • Field / natural experiments: causality + realism (but messy)
  • Observational studies: broad coverage (but confounding risks)
  • Qualitative / case evidence: mechanisms and context (weak on magnitude)

Your default should be: start with mechanism + prediction, then ask what evidence type can test it.

4) Always separate three things

Many confusions disappear if you keep these separate:

(i) Trait vs state vs skill

  • Trait: stable tendency
  • State: temporary condition (stress, fatigue)
  • Skill: learned capability (reappraisal, planning)

(ii) Preference vs cognition

Is the person choosing differently because they want something different, or because they represent/compute differently (attention, memory, framing)?

(iii) Bias vs noise

Is the pattern directional and systematic (bias), or just variability (noise)?

5) A minimal reading order (the “spine”)

If you want a clean progression:

  1. Cognition → attention, memory, working memory limits
  2. Learning → reinforcement, habit, feedback, adaptation
  3. Decision-making → risk, time, heuristics, choice architecture
  4. Emotion & motivation → threat, anger, shame/guilt, goal priority
  5. Social cognition → mentalizing, attribution, identity, norms
  6. Methods + measurement → what counts as evidence; validity/reliability

Then branch into Topics: - biases, norms & groups, moral psychology, relationships, development, etc.

6) How to take notes that remain useful

For every page you write, include:

  • Definitions (short and operational)
  • Mechanism (what actually runs in the mind/system)
  • Predictions (2–5 bullets)
  • Failure modes (when it won’t apply)
  • One canonical example (worked, not vague)
  • Interventions (what moves behavior and why)
  • Links to other pages (foundations ↔︎ topics ↔︎ overlaps)

If you do this, your notes won’t become a glossary.

7) Common pitfalls (and how to avoid them)

“Just-so” explanations

If the explanation can fit any outcome, it explains nothing. Fix: force counterfactual predictions (what would falsify it?).

Overgeneralizing from one study

Single results are fragile. Fix: look for replications and boundary conditions.

Confusing labels with mechanisms

“Self-control” or “trust” is not an explanation. Fix: specify the process (cue-triggered habit? threat appraisal? credibility heuristic?).

Ignoring measurement and selection

Especially with self-report and institutional data. Fix: ask how the variable is constructed; what’s missing; who is unobserved.
